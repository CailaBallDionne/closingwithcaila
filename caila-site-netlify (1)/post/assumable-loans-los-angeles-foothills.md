It reads as too good to be true. You're buying a house, and instead of taking out a fresh home loan at a 6 point something (cusping on 7) interest rate, you assume the remainder of the seller's mortgage with their sweet 3 percent rate, and take out the rest of what you need at the higher rate. Feels like a win all around, right?

It is a fair question, and it is not a myth. Assumable mortgages are real, they are federally sanctioned, and in the right transaction they are a powerful tool in a high-rate market. The catch is that the version circulating on social media skips the part that decides whether the whole thing works, and in Los Angeles that part is a very large number.

**An assumable loan lets a buyer take over the seller's existing mortgage at its original interest rate, balance and terms, instead of getting a new loan at today's rates. FHA, VA and USDA loans are generally assumable with lender approval and buyer credit qualification. Most conventional loans are not. The buyer must still cover the difference between the loan balance and the purchase price, which in high-priced markets is usually the deciding factor.**

Let's get into what that actually looks like here.

## Why This Is Even Worth Talking About

The 30-year fixed averaged 6.95% in Freddie Mac's survey for the week of September 17, 2026 ([Freddie Mac PMMS](https://www.freddiemac.com/pmms)). Meanwhile, roughly half of all outstanding mortgages in the country, 49.9% as of the first quarter of 2026, still carry a rate below 4% ([FHFA National Mortgage Database, via Calculated Risk](https://calculatedrisk.substack.com/p/fhfas-q1-national-mortgage-database)).

Sit with that for a second. Half the homeowners in America are sitting on debt that no lender will write today. That gap between what existing homeowners pay and what new buyers pay is the main reason inventory has been so stubborn, and it is also the reason an assumable loan is worth more than it was five years ago. You are not just buying a house. You are buying a financial instrument that no longer exists.

## Which Loans Can Actually Be Assumed

This is the first filter, and it eliminates most of the market before you start.

**FHA loans:** Assumable. For any FHA loan closed on or after December 15, 1989, the buyer has to be credit-qualified by the servicer, and that requirement lasts the entire life of the loan ([HUD Handbook 4155.1, Chapter 7](https://www.hud.gov/sites/documents/4155-1_7.pdf)). Assumable does not mean automatic. You still get underwritten.

**VA loans:** Assumable, and here is the part that surprises people: the buyer does not have to be a veteran. A civilian can assume a VA loan. Whether that is a good idea *for the seller* is a completely separate question, and we will come back to it, because it is a commonly botched piece of assumption advice out there.

**USDA loans:** Assumable with lender approval. Largely academic in our area, since USDA eligibility maps do not cover the Foothills, but worth knowing if your search ever drifts toward the Antelope Valley or the Central Coast.

**Conventional loans:** Almost never assumable. Nearly all of them contain a due-on-sale clause, which lets the lender demand the full balance the moment the property changes hands, and federal law has backed that up since the Garn-St Germain Act of 1982. A handful of adjustable-rate conventional loans are assumable by their own terms. If you hear otherwise about a fixed-rate conventional loan, the note itself is the only thing that settles it.

So realistically, when we talk about assumable loans, we are talking about a subset of FHA and VA loans. In a market where the FHA loan limit for Los Angeles County caps out at $1,249,125 for 2026, that already tells you something about which price points this tool lives in.

## The Equity Gap Is the Whole Ballgame

Here is what gets left out of the pitch. You assume the seller's *loan balance*, not the seller's *purchase price*. The seller still wants their equity, and they want all of it, in cash, at closing.

That difference between the sale price and the remaining loan balance is the equity gap, and it is the number that determines whether an assumption is a brilliant move or a non-starter.

Run it on a plausible Foothills scenario. Say a buyer purchased in 2021 with an FHA loan of $820,000 at 3.25%, which was right around the FHA ceiling at the time. Five years of payments later, that balance is down to roughly $732,000. The home is now worth $1,150,000.

- Purchase price: $1,150,000
- Assumable loan balance: $732,000
- **Equity gap the buyer has to cover: $418,000**

That is a conforming-loan-sized pile of money standing between you and that 3.25%. The rate is spectacular. The rate is also attached to only 64% of what you need.

This is why assumptions are structurally harder here than almost anywhere else in the country. The median sale price in [La Crescenta-Montrose](https://www.redfin.com/city/23645/CA/La-Crescenta-Montrose/housing-market) was $1.44 million over the three months ending July 2026, and [La Cañada Flintridge](https://www.redfin.com/city/9895/CA/La-Caada-Flintridge/housing-market) was $2.6 million, per Redfin. Appreciation that has been generous to Foothills sellers is exactly what inflates the gap for anyone trying to assume. The better the neighborhood has done, the worse the assumption math gets.

For a fuller picture of where local pricing sits, the [La Crescenta market update](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/la-crescenta-market-update-july-2026) has the recent numbers.

## How Buyers Actually Bridge the Gap

There are four real options, and most workable deals use some combination.

**Cash.** The cleanest and the least available. If you have $418,000 sitting around, you have a very easy transaction and probably a different set of questions.

**A second mortgage behind the assumed first.** This is the structure that makes assumptions possible for buyers who are not writing a half-million-dollar check. The assumed loan stays in first position, a new subordinate lien covers the gap, and you end up with two payments and a blended cost of borrowing.

On the VA side this got meaningfully clearer in August 2024, when the VA issued [Circular 26-24-17](https://www.benefits.va.gov/HOMELOANS/documents/circulars/26-24-17.pdf) spelling out how secondary borrowing on an assumption is supposed to be handled. The short version: the second lien has to be subordinate to the VA loan, the proceeds can only go toward seller equity and allowable closing costs, the assumer cannot walk away with cash back, and the second's monthly payment counts against debt-to-income. Your lender is the one who confirms what any of that means for your file.

**Seller carryback.** The seller finances the gap themselves and holds a note. Terms are whatever the two parties agree to, which makes this the most flexible option on paper. In practice it depends entirely on a seller who does not need all of their equity immediately, which is rare when the reason they are selling is that they are buying something else.

**Gift funds.** If family help is part of your picture, an assumption is one of the higher-leverage places to deploy it, precisely because the gap is the binding constraint. The documentation rules are strict and worth understanding before the money moves, which is what the [gift funds and gift letters post](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/down-payment-gift-funds-california) covers in detail.

## The Math, Honestly

Same scenario. Purchase price $1,150,000, assumed FHA first of $732,000 at 3.25%, buyer brings 10% cash ($115,000), and a second lien of roughly $303,000 covers the rest.

Assume, with a 30-year second at 8.5%:

- Assumed first, principal and interest: $3,569
- Inherited FHA mortgage insurance: about $376
- Second lien: $2,327
- **Total: roughly $6,272 per month**

New financing on the same house, 10% down, at 6.95%:

- Loan of $1,035,000, principal and interest: $6,851
- FHA mortgage insurance: about $376
- **Total: roughly $7,327 per month**

The assumption wins by about $1,055 a month, and the blended cost of the two liens works out around 4.8%. That is a real, material advantage, and it is why this structure is worth understanding rather than dismissing.

Now watch what happens when one variable moves. Second liens are frequently written on 15-year terms rather than 30. At 15 years, that same $303,000 second costs $2,981 a month instead of $2,327, and the total lands at roughly $6,925. The advantage shrinks from $1,055 to about $400.

Same house. Same glorious 3.25% first. One term change and most of the benefit evaporates.

That is the thing to internalize. The headline rate is not the deal. The structure of the gap financing is the deal, and it is the part nobody puts in the Instagram caption. Every figure above is illustrative, calculated on standard amortization, and your actual numbers depend entirely on what a lender will write for you.

## The Approval Ceiling Nobody Mentions

Two constraints tend to kill these deals, and both live with the second-lien lender rather than with the servicer holding the assumable loan.

**Combined loan-to-value.** Second-lien lenders generally want the two loans together to stay somewhere in the 85% to 90% range of value. The example above sits at exactly 90%, which is the outer edge. If the appraisal comes in even slightly under the purchase price, the second shrinks and the cash requirement grows to fill the hole.

**Debt-to-income with both payments counted.** You get underwritten on the combined obligation, not the assumed one. Plenty of buyers qualify comfortably for the assumption alone and then fail once the second lien's payment is added in.

Both of these are lender determinations, not agent determinations, and they are the first things to nail down in writing. Ballpark reassurance from anyone who is not underwriting your loan is worth nothing at closing. If you are still sorting out where you stand generally, [pre-approval versus pre-qualification](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/pre-approval-vs-pre-qualification) explains what an actual approval requires.

## FHA Assumptions: Inherit the Rate, Inherit the MIP

When you assume an FHA loan you take the whole package, including the mortgage insurance, on its original terms.

For FHA loans with case numbers assigned on or after June 3, 2013, the annual mortgage insurance premium runs for the full 30 years of the term when the original loan-to-value was above 90%, meaning less than 10% down ([HUD Mortgagee Letter 13-04](https://www.hud.gov/sites/documents/13-04ml.pdf)). Since the vast majority of FHA buyers put down 3.5%, most assumable FHA loans out there carry insurance that does not fall off on its own.

Two details worth pinning down before you fall in love with the rate:

The MIP clock runs from the original closing, not from yours. If the seller is five years in, you inherit 25 years of it, not a fresh 30. That is a point in your favor.

And it is not a rounding error. A few hundred dollars a month over two-plus decades is real money, and it belongs in the comparison against new financing rather than in a footnote. Your lender can pull the exact premium and the exact remaining duration from the servicer's assumption package. Do not estimate this one.

## VA Assumptions: The Part That Gets Sellers Hurt

This is where I want to be very direct, because the most widely repeated claim about VA assumptions is wrong, and it is wrong in a way that costs sellers their benefit.

You will read that a non-veteran can assume a VA loan and the seller's entitlement is restored. The first half is true. The second half is not.

When a non-veteran assumes a VA loan, the seller's VA entitlement stays tied to that mortgage until it is paid off. The seller does not get it back. They cannot use their VA benefit to buy their next home with zero down, because it is still sitting in a house they no longer own, attached to a loan a stranger is now paying. If that buyer defaults, the seller can lose the entitlement outright.

Getting the entitlement released requires a substitution of entitlement, which means the buyer has to be an eligible veteran willing to swap their own entitlement in, documented on VA Form 26-8106, plus a lender-executed release of liability for the seller. Veteran seller, veteran buyer. Otherwise the seller is trading their future VA benefit for a faster sale, and they deserve to understand that trade before they agree to market the loan as assumable.

The costs on a VA assumption are comparatively modest: a VA funding fee of 0.5% of the loan balance on transfer, with the usual exemptions for veterans receiving compensation for a service-connected disability, plus a lender processing charge capped at $300 for lenders with automatic authority or $250 otherwise, in each case plus the actual cost of a credit report.

## "Seller Is Released" Is Not Automatic Either

Worth stating plainly, because it applies to both programs. The seller is only off the hook when the lender executes a formal release of liability. That release depends on the buyer being approved as creditworthy. If an assumption somehow closes without one, the original borrower can remain on the debt for a house they have sold, which is roughly the worst outcome in residential real estate.

Anyone on either side of an assumption should treat written confirmation of the release as a closing condition, not a formality.

## Where This Actually Pencils Out in LA

I am not going to tell you assumable loans are the answer for the median Foothills buyer, because the gap math above says they usually are not. But there are specific situations where this is the best available structure:

**Lower price points where the gap stays manageable.** Condos and townhomes, and the more attainable corners of Glendale and Northeast LA. A $700,000 purchase against a $520,000 assumed balance leaves a $180,000 gap, which is a very different conversation than $418,000. That $520,000 at 3.25% runs $2,263 a month versus $3,442 at today's rates, and the gap is small enough that ordinary down payment savings plus a modest second can close it.

**Homes purchased with FHA or VA financing between 2020 and 2022.** This is the actual target. Rates bottomed out then, which means the loans worth assuming were written in a narrow window. A listing agent who knows to advertise it is doing their seller a favor, and a buyer's agent who knows to ask is doing the same.

**Buyers with strong cash but imperfect timing.** If you have significant liquidity from a stock event or a previous sale but you are buying into a rate environment you hate, the assumption is a way to convert cash into a payment nobody can write for you today.

**Slower-moving listings.** An assumption takes longer than a conventional close because the servicer controls the timeline and servicers are not famous for urgency. On a Foothills listing getting four offers in a weekend, "my offer is contingent on a loan assumption approval" is a competitive liability, not a selling point. On a property that has been sitting 30-plus days, a seller may be quite happy to wait.

That last point is the one I would underline. In our market the constraint on assumptions is often not the money at all. It is the calendar.

## What to Do If You Want to Pursue One

The process, in the order it actually happens:

1. **Confirm the loan is assumable, in writing, from the servicer.** Not from the listing, not from the listing agent's recollection. The note and the servicer's assumption package are the only authorities.
2. **Get the real numbers.** Current balance, rate, remaining term, exact monthly mortgage insurance and how long it lasts, and the servicer's assumption fee.
3. **Calculate the gap and solve for it before you write.** This is the step people skip, and it is the one that determines whether there is a deal at all.
4. **Line up gap financing in parallel, not after.** Your second-lien lender and the servicer need to be working at the same time, with subordination handled on the front end.
5. **Get credit-qualified by the servicer.** Assumption underwriting is typically lighter than a full origination, but it is underwriting.
6. **Write the assumption approval into the contract as a contingency.** If the servicer denies you, you need a way out with your deposit intact. This is exactly the kind of protection [contingencies](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/what-are-real-estate-contingencies) exist for.
7. **Confirm the seller's release of liability**, and on a VA loan, settle the entitlement question before anyone signs anything.

## If an Assumption Is Not on the Table

Most of the time it will not be, simply because the assumable loan has to already exist on a house you actually want. The good news is that assumptions are not the only way to attack a rate.

A seller-funded rate buydown does something structurally similar, lowering your payment without touching the purchase price, and it can be negotiated on any listing rather than only the rare one with the right loan attached. That is covered in [the buydown post](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/interest-rate-buydown-programs-explained). And if you are trying to buy before you sell, [down payment options for that situation](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/buying-before-you-sell-down-payment-options) work through the equity-access side of it.

The broader point is that "wait for rates to drop" is not the only alternative to paying today's rate. There are three or four levers, and which one applies depends on the specific house and the specific seller.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is an assumable mortgage?

An assumable mortgage lets a buyer take over the seller's existing home loan at its original interest rate, remaining balance and terms, rather than getting a new loan at current rates. The lender or servicer must approve the assumption and the buyer must be credit-qualified. The seller is released from the debt only when the lender executes a formal release of liability.

### Which types of loans are assumable?

FHA, VA and USDA loans are generally assumable with lender approval and buyer credit qualification. Most conventional loans are not, because nearly all contain a due-on-sale clause allowing the lender to demand full repayment when the property transfers. Some adjustable-rate conventional loans are assumable by their own terms, so the note itself is the only reliable answer.

### Do I need to be a veteran to assume a VA loan?

No. A non-veteran can assume a VA loan with lender approval. However, when a non-veteran assumes, the seller's VA entitlement remains tied to that loan until it is paid off, so the seller cannot reuse their VA benefit on a future purchase. Releasing the seller's entitlement requires a substitution of entitlement, which means the buyer must be an eligible veteran substituting their own entitlement.

### How do I cover the difference between the loan balance and the purchase price?

That difference is called the equity gap, and buyers cover it with cash, a second mortgage subordinate to the assumed loan, seller financing, gift funds, or some combination. Second-lien lenders typically want the combined loan-to-value to stay in the 85% to 90% range, and the second loan's payment counts toward your debt-to-income ratio during underwriting.

### Do I inherit the seller's FHA mortgage insurance?

Yes. Assuming an FHA loan means taking on its mortgage insurance on the original terms. For FHA loans with case numbers assigned on or after June 3, 2013 where the original loan-to-value was above 90%, the annual premium runs for the full 30 years of the term. The clock runs from the original closing date, not from your assumption, so you inherit whatever time is left rather than a fresh 30 years.

### Are assumable loans practical in Los Angeles?

They can be, but the equity gap is the limiting factor. With median prices around $1.44 million in La Crescenta-Montrose and $2.6 million in La Cañada Flintridge, an assumable balance often covers well under three-quarters of the purchase price, leaving a large gap to finance. Assumptions tend to work best at lower price points, on FHA and VA loans originated between roughly 2020 and 2022, and on listings where the seller can accommodate a longer servicer-controlled timeline.

### How long does a loan assumption take?

Assumption underwriting is usually lighter than a full origination, but the timeline belongs to the servicer rather than to a lender you chose, so it is generally less predictable than a standard close. That unpredictability is why assumptions are a harder sell on competitive listings and more workable on properties that have been on the market longer. Your lender and the servicer can give you their current processing estimates.

## The Bottom Line

Assumable loans are not a loophole and they are not a myth. They are a narrow, powerful tool that works when three things line up at once: the house has the right loan on it, the equity gap is small enough to finance, and the seller can live with a timeline they do not control.

In the Foothills, the second condition is usually the one that fails, and the honest answer is often that a negotiated buydown gets you further than hunting for an assumable listing. But "usually" is not "always," and the difference between the two is worth knowing about a house before you write on it rather than after.
