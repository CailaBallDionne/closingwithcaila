You did it! After weeks, months, or even years of searching for the one, and 30-ish days of an [inspection-filled escrow](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/what-happens-after-offer-accepted-california-escrow-timeline), you own your house. Congrats! But much like the "wait...you're sending me home without an instruction manual or adult supervision? Is that a good idea?" feeling I had when I took my newborn daughter home from the hospital, you have now transitioned from the well managed care of your Realtor to being the homeowner in charge.

...and you're not even sure where the water shutoff is.

First of all: Don't panic. This is completely normal. While closing is the finish line of the buying process, it is also the starting line of owning a home. The first month is when a handful of small tasks matter more than they look. Some keep you safe, a couple have real deadlines, and one is a tax bill that's going to hit your new mailbox soon.

**In your first 30 days as a California homeowner, rekey the locks, find your water, gas, and electrical shutoffs, test your smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, update your address with the DMV within 10 days, file for the homeowners' exemption, and watch your mailbox for a supplemental property tax bill that your lender may not pay for you.**

Here is the checklist in the order I would tackle it. I have also noted which items are actual rules, which have deadlines, and which are just good homeowner habits.

## Day One: The Safety Basics

**Rekey or change the locks.** You cannot know who has copies of the keys to a home you just bought, whether it is a past owner, a contractor, or a neighbor who once watered the plants. This is a good habit, not a rule.

**Find your shutoffs.** Locate the main water shutoff, the gas meter shutoff, and the electrical panel. Your inspection report may have pointed some of these out, so check it first. If your gas comes from SoCalGas, [the company advises knowing where your meter is and keeping a 12-inch or larger adjustable wrench nearby](https://prnewswire.com/news-releases/socalgas-offers-emergency-preparedness-tips-for-great-shakeout-279501772.html). It also says to shut off the gas only if you smell gas, hear it escaping, or see other signs of a leak, and to call the utility to turn it back on instead of doing it yourself.

**Test the smoke and carbon monoxide alarms.** This one has a rule behind it. [California requires carbon monoxide alarms in single-family homes with an attached garage or a fossil fuel source, and battery-only smoke alarms sold now must have a sealed 10-year battery](https://sf-fire.org/node/235). The testing part is a habit, and [CAL FIRE recommends testing alarms monthly and replacing smoke alarms every 10 years](https://www.lakeconews.com/news/16781-daylight-saving-ends-sunday-officials-urge-californians-to-change-clocks-smoke-alarm-batteries). Check the manufacture date printed on each alarm so you know how old it is.

**Photograph everything before you move in.** A quick walk-through with your phone documents the home's condition on day one, and it gives you a starting point for a home inventory later. This is a habit, and one you will be glad to have if you ever file an insurance claim.

**Confirm your utilities.** Make sure power, gas, water, trash, and internet are set up in your name. Which companies serve you can differ by city and even by street, so if something is not obvious, your agent can point you in the right direction.

## Within 10 Days: Update Your Address

This is an actual rule. [The California DMV says that if you move, you must notify it of your new address within ten days](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/handbook/california-driver-handbook/changing-replacing-and-renewing-your-drivers-license/). You can submit the change online at dmv.ca.gov/addresschange or by mail.

While you are at it, set up mail forwarding with the USPS, and update your address with your employer, your banks, and your insurance companies. Those are habits, and they are easy to forget until a bill goes to the wrong house.

## First Two Weeks: The Paperwork

**Save your closing documents.** Keep your final Closing Disclosure, the recorded deed when you receive it, your loan documents, your title policy, your inspection reports, and the seller disclosures in one place, both digital and paper. Your CPA will likely want some of them at tax time, so ask which ones to keep.

**Read your homeowners insurance policy.** Your policy should already be in force from closing, but the declarations page is worth a careful read: your coverage limits, your deductible, and what is and is not covered. Your insurance agent is the right person for questions about specifics. If your home is in or near a fire hazard zone, my post on [homeowners insurance in the Foothills](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/foothills-homeowners-insurance-after-eaton-fire) explains what to ask.

**Confirm your first mortgage payment.** Your closing paperwork lists the due date and who your servicer is. Ask your lender how to set up payments, and if you ever get a notice that your loan servicing is moving to a different company, confirm it with your lender before you act on it.

**Start your maintenance list.** Pull out your inspection report and list everything it flagged, then rank it, with safety and water issues first. A written list is easier to chip away at than a vague feeling that the house needs things.

**If you bought into an HOA,** collect your keys, fobs, and parking information, and ask about move-in rules. Associations often have rules about elevators and parking. My [guide to buying a condo](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/buying-a-condo-los-angeles-hoa-guide) covers what else to keep on hand.

## Within 30 Days: The Tax Items

### The supplemental property tax bill

California reassesses a property when ownership changes, and [state law sets the reassessment as of the first day of the month after the change](https://www.propertytax.lacounty.gov/Home/SupplementalSecuredProperty). The result is a supplemental tax bill, which is separate from your regular annual bill and is prorated for the rest of the tax year. My post on [Prop 13 and Prop 19](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/prop-13-lock-in-effect-selling-california) explains why the reassessment happens.

Two things surprise new owners. First, the timing: the bill can take weeks or months to arrive, depending on how busy the county offices are, and the due dates printed on it depend on when it is mailed. Second, the payer: [LA County's Treasurer and Tax Collector tells owners not to expect their lender to pay supplemental bills](https://ttc.lacounty.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/TOP-FIVE-DOS-AND-DONTS-INSERT.pdf), and to contact the lender about them.

So ask your lender early how supplemental bills are handled, and set aside money for it. How much it is depends on your price and the property's previous assessed value, and your lender or the Assessor's Office can help you estimate it.

### The homeowners' exemption

If the home is your principal residence, you can claim an exemption that lowers your assessed value by $7,000. [The state Board of Equalization describes the claim process and the February 15 deadline for the full exemption](https://aws.boe.ca.gov/pdf/pub800-6.pdf), and [LA County says new property owners automatically receive a claim form by mail](https://res.cloudinary.com/los-angeles-county-assessor/image/upload/v1622578758/Factsheet/Home_Owners_Exemption_Factsheet_EN.pdf). The county factsheet also says the form must be mailed back, since sensitive documents are not accepted electronically.

The savings are modest. [The LA County Assessor has put them at roughly $70 a year](https://www.nbclosangeles.com/news/homeowners-exemption-la-county-assessor/20858/). It is not required, but it is a few minutes of paperwork for savings that renew every year, so put the form on your calendar.

## Watch Your Mailbox

New owners get a lot of mail, and some of it is designed to look official. [LA County's Registrar-Recorder warns that homeowners receive official-looking letters urging them to request a copy of their grant deed](https://lavote.gov/Documents/notice-to-homeowners-mail-solicitation-alert.pdf), and says you can get a copy directly from the county without a third party. If a letter asks for money, check with the county before you pay.

You can also [sign up for LA County's property owner e-notification alerts](https://dcba.lacounty.gov/?p=33628), which email you when a document is recorded against your property. Signing up is a good habit.

One more caution from the escrow side: if anyone ever contacts you about changing where your mortgage payment goes, verify it by phone with your lender using a number you already trust.

## If You Bought in the Foothills

Many homes in the area sit on hillsides or in or near fire hazard zones, depending on the exact address. In your first month, take a walk around the property and look at where water drains, the condition of any retaining walls, and the vents and roof. [California's Safer from Wildfires regulation lists mitigation steps that insurers must recognize with discounts](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/200-wrr/Safer-from-Wildfires.cfm), so it is worth asking your insurance agent what applies. If you want a refresher on what to look for outside, my [open house checklist](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/what-to-look-for-at-an-open-house-foothills) covers it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I rekey the locks after buying a house?

It is a good habit, not a legal requirement. You cannot know how many copies of the current keys exist or who has them, and rekeying the existing locks is a common first step for new owners.

### Will my lender pay my supplemental property tax bill?

Not necessarily. The Los Angeles County Treasurer and Tax Collector says not to expect your lender to pay supplemental tax bills and to contact your lender about them, so confirm with your lender how yours will be handled.

### How soon do I have to update my address with the DMV after buying a home?

According to the California DMV, you must notify the DMV of your new address within ten days of moving. You can submit the change online at dmv.ca.gov/addresschange or by mail.

### Do I have to apply for the homeowners' exemption in Los Angeles County?

Yes, you need to file a claim. The LA County Assessor says new property owners automatically receive a claim form by mail. Filing by February 15 gets the full $7,000 reduction in assessed value, and the exemption applies to your principal residence.

## The Bottom Line

None of this is difficult, but a lot of it has a deadline or a surprise attached, and the first month is when it all lands at once. Do the safety items on day one, the DMV in the first ten days, and put the supplemental bill and the homeowners' exemption on your calendar. The rest can follow.
