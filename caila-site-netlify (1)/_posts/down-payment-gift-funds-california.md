I am not going to pretend we did this on our own.

When my husband and I bought our first house, we were buying in a market where the good listings had five offers on them by the second weekend. Getting an offer taken seriously meant showing up with real money down and financing that did not make a listing agent nervous. Could we have gotten there on what we had saved alone? Maybe eventually, on some house. Almost certainly not on that house, in that market, with an offer that actually competed. What made it work was our families, who helped in a way that a lot of first-time buyers get help and then feel weird about admitting.

So let's take the weird out of it. Family gift money is one of the most common ways people get into their first home, especially here. It is also one of the most common ways a clean file gets delayed, because the money comes with rules attached and nobody tells the buyer until the paperwork is already due.

**A down payment gift is money given to a buyer with no expectation of repayment, and lenders will require a signed gift letter plus a documented paper trail showing the funds moving from the donor's account to yours. Most loan programs let a gift cover your entire down payment on a primary residence. For 2026, a donor can give any one person up to $19,000 without filing a gift tax return, or $38,000 from a married couple, and California has no state gift tax of its own. The tax limits and the lender's rules are two completely separate things, and the lender's rules are the ones that can actually delay your closing.**

That last sentence is the part I wish someone would put on a billboard. Let's take it apart.

## A Gift Means a Gift. That Is Not a Formality.

The defining feature of a gift, in the eyes of every loan program, is that you do not have to pay it back. HUD's handbook puts it about as plainly as a government document can, defining gifts as contributions of cash or equity "with no expectation of repayment."

This matters because underwriters are not evaluating your family's intentions. They are evaluating your debt. If the money is actually a loan from your parents, that is a monthly obligation that changes your debt-to-income ratio, and a loan dressed up as a gift is misrepresentation on a federal loan application. I mention this not because I think you are planning to commit fraud, but because "they said they'd pay us back eventually when they can" is a real conversation families have, and it needs to get resolved before anyone signs a letter.

If your family wants to be repaid, there are legitimate ways to structure that. Tell your lender up front and let them tell you what it does to your numbers. What you cannot do is call it a gift on paper and a loan in practice.

## Who Is Actually Allowed to Give You the Money

Broader than people assume, with one hard line running through all of it.

On conventional loans, [Fannie Mae's guidelines](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-4.3-04/personal-gifts) allow gifts from a relative, defined as "the borrower's spouse, child, or other dependent, or by any other individual who is related to the borrower by blood, marriage, adoption, or legal guardianship." They also allow gifts from "a domestic partner (or relative of the domestic partner), individual engaged to marry the borrower, former relative, or an individual with a long-standing familial-like or mentorship relationship." That last category is more generous than most buyers expect. A godparent, a mentor, the aunt who is not technically your aunt, these can work with the right documentation.

FHA casts a similarly wide net, allowing family members, employers, labor unions, charitable organizations, governmental agencies running homeownership programs, and close friends with a clearly defined and documented interest in the borrower. VA loans take a similar approach and impose no dollar limit on the gift at all.

Here is the hard line, and it is the same across every program: **the donor cannot be anyone with a financial interest in the transaction.** Fannie Mae's language is that the donor "may not be, or have any affiliation with, the builder, the developer, the real estate agent, or any other interested party to the transaction." That means the seller, the builder, the listing agent, and yes, me. If a seller wants to help with your costs, that is a seller concession, which is a different mechanism with its own limits, and your lender will treat it completely differently.

The other quiet rule worth knowing: the money has to be the donor's money. It cannot come from a payday loan, a credit card cash advance, or any other non-collateralized loan. If your parents' bank statement shows a mystery deposit landing three days before they wired you the gift, the underwriter is going to ask where that came from too. Sourcing does not stop at your account.

Your specific lender may layer on additional requirements beyond program minimums, which the industry calls overlays, and those vary from lender to lender. Ask yours early rather than assuming the published guideline is the whole story.

## What a Gift Letter Has to Say

This is the document everyone hears about and nobody sees until the day it is due. The good news is that it is short and the requirements are consistent across loan types. A gift letter has to include the donor's name, address, and phone number, their relationship to you, the dollar amount, a clear statement that no repayment is expected, and the donor's signature.

That is it. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac will even accept a letter stating the maximum amount of the gift rather than the exact dollar figure, which is useful when the final number is still moving.

Your lender will almost certainly hand you their own gift letter form, and if they do, use theirs. But it helps enormously to know what is coming, especially if the conversation with your family is going to take a minute. Here is a template you can send them ahead of time so nobody is scrambling three days before closing.

```
GIFT LETTER

Date: _______________

Donor name(s): ______________________________________

Borrower name(s): ___________________________________

Relationship of donor to borrower: __________________
(for example: mother, grandfather, aunt, godparent)

Gift amount: $_______________________________________

Property address: ___________________________________

_____________________________________________________

This is a bona fide gift. No repayment of any kind is
expected or implied, now or in the future. No part of
this gift has been borrowed from any person or entity
with an interest in the sale of the property, including
the seller, the builder, the real estate agent or
broker, or any other interested party.

Source of the gift funds: ___________________________
(for example: personal checking account ending in 1234
at XYZ Bank)

Funds will be transferred on or about: ______________

DONOR
Print name: _________________________________________
Signature: __________________________________________
Date: _______________________________________________
Address: ____________________________________________
Phone: ______________________________________________

SECOND DONOR (if the gift is from a married couple)
Print name: _________________________________________
Signature: __________________________________________
Date: _______________________________________________

BORROWER
Print name: _________________________________________
Signature: __________________________________________
Date: _______________________________________________
```

Two notes on using it. If the gift is coming from a married couple giving jointly, both of them should sign. And do not sign a letter until the amount is close to settled, because if the letter says one number and the wire says another, your lender will send it back.

## When the Money Needs to Be in Your Account

Buyers tend to think there is a magic waiting period, that money has to sit in an account for some number of months before it counts. That idea comes from something real. Lenders typically review two months of bank statements on a purchase, which is where the 60-day figure people repeat comes from, and money that has been sitting in your account across that whole window generally reads as your own funds without further questions.

But gift money does not work on a clock. Properly documented gift funds do not need to season. A gift that landed last week is fine if the letter and the paper trail are in order. A gift that landed four months ago is still a gift, and if the underwriter spots the deposit, they will still want it documented.

What you actually need to avoid is an undocumented large deposit. Fannie Mae defines a large deposit as "a single deposit that exceeds 50% of the total monthly qualifying income for the loan," and on purchase transactions the rule is unambiguous: if those funds are needed to complete the purchase, "the lender must document that those funds are from an acceptable source." An unexplained five-figure deposit sitting in your statements is not a neutral event. It is a question the underwriter has to answer before they can clear your file.

So the practical timing advice, in order of preference:

1. **Best case: talk to your lender before the money moves at all.** Tell them a gift is coming, how much, and from whom. They will tell you exactly how they want it transferred and will often prefer to have the letter signed first.

2. **Move the money by traceable transfer.** Wire or a bank-to-bank electronic transfer is cleanest. A check works if you keep the deposit slip. Cash does not work. Neither does a stack of Venmo transactions, which is a real thing buyers try and a real thing that creates a mess.

3. **Give it a couple of weeks of runway before closing.** Not because the money needs to age, but because you and the donor both need time to pull statements, sign the letter, and answer whatever follow-up the underwriter asks. Two to three weeks ahead of closing is comfortable.

4. **Alternatively, skip your account entirely.** Gift funds can be wired straight to escrow. Fannie Mae accepts "a copy of the donor's check to the closing agent" or "a settlement statement showing receipt of the donor's check" as verification. This is sometimes the simplest path, though it needs to be coordinated with your lender and escrow well in advance, not improvised on closing day.

Whatever you do, do not move the money and then tell your lender about it. The same discipline applies here that applies to [everything else between pre-approval and closing](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/pre-approval-vs-pre-qualification): tell your lender before, not after.

## The Paper Trail: Both Sides, Not Just Yours

Buyers are often surprised that the donor has homework too. Verification means showing the money leaving one account and arriving in another, which takes documents from both parties.

Depending on how the transfer happens, your lender will typically want some combination of: evidence of the electronic transfer, a copy of the donor's check along with your deposit slip, and the donor's bank statement showing the withdrawal. Lenders also need evidence the donor withdrew the funds from their own account if the check or money order does not show the donor's name as the remitter, a point spelled out in [this lender guide to gift documentation](https://www.uhmgo.com/contentimages/706405964/Getting_the_Gift_Right_Guide.pdf).

It is worth warning your family about this part in advance, because "we need a page of your bank statement" lands very differently when it arrives as a surprise on a Tuesday than when they have known it was coming for a month. Donors are typically only asked for the portion showing the withdrawal, not their entire financial life, and that framing helps.

## The 2026 Tax Numbers, and Why They Probably Do Not Apply to You

Here is the piece that makes families nervous for no reason.

For 2026, the federal annual gift tax exclusion is **$19,000 per recipient**. A married couple can give **$38,000** to the same person, because each spouse has their own exclusion. Gifts under that threshold do not require a gift tax return at all.

Here is how this multiplies faster than most buyers expect. The exclusion is per donor, per recipient, per year. So if your parents are married and you are married, that is four separate gifts: your mother to you, your mother to your spouse, your father to you, and your father to your spouse. At $19,000 apiece, that is **$76,000 in 2026 without anyone filing a gift tax return.** Nothing clever is happening there. It is just how the exclusion is structured.

Above those thresholds, the thing that happens is usually just paperwork. The donor files IRS Form 709, and the excess counts against their lifetime exemption rather than generating a tax bill. That lifetime exemption is **$15 million per person for 2026**, raised under Public Law 119-21 and now roughly $30 million for a married couple. Unless your family is doing estate planning at a scale where they already have a dedicated attorney, going over $19,000 means a form, not a tax.

Also worth knowing: **the donor is the one responsible for gift tax, not you.** The IRS is explicit that "the donor is generally responsible for paying the gift tax." Gift money is not income to you, and you do not report it on your return.

And here is where California simplifies things. **California levies no gift tax, no estate tax, and no inheritance tax of its own, which means the federal rules above are the only ones that apply.** That is not the same thing as gift money being tax-free here. It means there is no second, state-level layer stacked on top of the federal system. Your parents in Glendale face exactly the same federal rules as your parents in Ohio, and nothing extra.

I am an agent, not a CPA or a tax attorney. If your family is giving a large gift or is in the middle of any kind of estate planning, that conversation belongs with their tax professional, not with me. But for a typical down payment gift, the tax side is far less complicated than the lending side.

## The California Wrinkles That Actually Matter

**Our numbers are bigger, so the gifts are bigger.** The 2026 conforming and FHA loan limits in Los Angeles County both sit at $1,249,125, well above the $832,750 national baseline, because we are classified as a high-cost area. That is an accurate reflection of what homes cost here, and it means a 20% down payment in La Crescenta or La Cañada Flintridge can easily be a six-figure gift. Crossing $19,000 is normal here. Plan for the Form 709 conversation rather than trying to structure around it.

**Gift money is often what makes an assumable loan workable.** If you are looking at a home with an [assumable FHA or VA loan](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/assumable-loans-los-angeles-foothills), the obstacle is almost never the interest rate. It is the gap between the seller's remaining loan balance and the purchase price, which has to be covered with cash, a second lien, or gifted funds. At local prices that gap is routinely six figures. Everything in this post about donor eligibility, sourcing and documentation applies exactly the same way.

**Splitting a gift across two calendar years is a legitimate option, but check the math first.** Families sometimes give part of the gift in December and part in January to stay under the annual exclusion twice. That is fine. Just make sure it does not collide with your actual closing timeline, and remember that the December money will show up in your bank statements as its own deposit needing its own letter.

**Gifting the house itself is a different animal entirely.** If the conversation with your family is less "here is money for a down payment" and more "what if you just took over the house," you are in property tax territory and the rules changed significantly with Proposition 19. That deserves its own treatment, which is [here](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/prop-19-parent-child-property-tax-transfer-california). Do not assume a parent-to-child transfer keeps the old assessed value anymore, because in most cases it does not.

**A gift of equity is the middle path.** If a family member is selling you their property, they can gift you a portion of their equity instead of cash, which becomes a credit at closing. Fannie Mae permits this toward down payment and closing costs on a primary residence or second home, though not toward reserves, and it still requires a gift letter plus a settlement statement documenting it. Usefully, an acceptable donor giving a gift of equity is not treated as an interested party, so the usual contribution limits do not choke it.

**Gift money can do more than lower your loan amount.** It can go toward closing costs, and on many programs toward discount points, which means it is worth asking your lender whether some of it is better spent on [buying down your rate](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/interest-rate-buydown-programs-explained) than on a slightly larger down payment. That comparison depends entirely on your numbers, your loan program, and how long you plan to stay, so run it with your loan officer before deciding.

## What Actually Goes Wrong

The failures here are almost never about the rules being harsh. They are about timing and communication.

- The buyer moves the money months early, never mentions it, and the deposit surfaces during underwriting as an unsourced question mark.

- The gift comes in as cash or as a series of app transfers, and there is no clean way to document it.

- The donor's own account shows an unexplained deposit right before the gift, and now there are two sourcing problems instead of one.

- The letter says $40,000 and the wire says $42,500.

- The family intends a loan, calls it a gift, and nobody addresses it until the underwriter asks a direct question.

- Everyone waits until the week of closing, and then the donor is traveling, or at a funeral, or simply does not check email, and a signature nobody thought about holds up a transaction.

Every one of these is preventable with a single conversation early.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can gift money cover my entire down payment?

On most programs, for a primary residence, yes. FHA allows the full 3.5% minimum down payment to come from an acceptable gift, and on conventional loans Fannie Mae requires no minimum borrower contribution for a one-unit principal residence, meaning the entire down payment can be gifted. Two- to four-unit properties and second homes above 80% LTV are different, requiring a 5% minimum contribution from the borrower's own funds. Your lender may apply stricter requirements than the program minimum, so confirm with them.

### Do I have to pay taxes on gift money for a down payment?

No. Gift money is not taxable income to the recipient, and you do not report it on your tax return. Gift tax, when it applies at all, is the donor's responsibility. For 2026, a donor can give up to $19,000 per recipient without filing anything, and amounts above that generally just require IRS Form 709 and count against a $15 million lifetime exemption rather than creating an actual tax bill.

### How long does gift money need to be in my bank account before closing?

There is no required seasoning period for properly documented gift funds. The 60-day figure buyers often hear comes from the two months of bank statements lenders typically review, not from a rule about gifts. What matters is the signed gift letter and a clear paper trail showing the transfer. Practically, moving the money two to three weeks before closing leaves comfortable room for documentation.

### Who is not allowed to give me a down payment gift?

Anyone with a financial interest in the transaction. That includes the seller, the builder or developer, the real estate agent or broker, and anyone affiliated with them. Seller contributions are still possible, but they are handled as seller concessions under a separate set of limits, not as gifts.

### Can my parents just wire the money straight to escrow?

Yes, and sometimes that is the cleanest option, since it keeps the deposit out of your bank statements entirely. Fannie Mae accepts a copy of the donor's check to the closing agent, or a settlement statement showing receipt of it, as verification. Coordinate it with your lender and escrow well ahead of closing rather than arranging it at the last minute.

### Does California charge a gift tax on down payment help?

California does not impose a gift tax, estate tax, or inheritance tax of its own, so there is no state-level layer on top of the federal rules. Federal gift tax rules still apply in California exactly as they do everywhere else. For most down payment gifts that means a filing requirement at most rather than a tax bill, since amounts above the annual exclusion generally just count against the donor's lifetime exemption.

## The Bottom Line

Family help is not cheating and it is not something to be cagey about. It is how an enormous number of people, including me, got into their first home, particularly in a market where saving a Foothills down payment from scratch is a brutal proposition.

What it is, is a documented transaction. Get the gift letter signed early, move the money in a way a bank can trace, tell your lender before it happens instead of after, and warn your family that they will be asked for a bank statement. Do those four things and the gift is a non-event in your file, which is exactly what you want it to be.
