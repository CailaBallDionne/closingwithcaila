A couple of months ago I opened my homeowners insurance renewal, looked at the number, closed it, and opened it again, because surely I'd misread it. I had not. My renewal premium was just over $18,000 a year.

For a house. In La Crescenta. That had not changed a bit since last year's renewal, which was about a fifth of the cost. My premium had gone up more than fivefold in a single year.

What. The. F...ortunate break that I have an insurance agent who I love.

I gave her a call, thankful she doesn't charge hourly for the therapy she provides, and we spent real time pulling it apart. The fix wasn't one magic cheaper policy. It was a restructure: a California FAIR Plan policy to cover fire, layered with a regular homeowners policy that carves out fire and covers everything else. Once both were added together, I was paying just under $5,000 a year. That's about a quarter of the renewal quote. Still more than I paid the year before, but a far cry from the panic-inducing figure enclosed in my renewal.

I'm telling you this not just because everyone loves an uplifting story about the unbreakable bond between a homeowner and her insurance agent, but because this is a reality you want to prepare yourself for if you're thinking of buying in the Foothills to avoid an ugly escrow surprise.

**Since the January 2025 Eaton Fire, homeowners insurance in La Crescenta, La Cañada Flintridge and the surrounding Foothills has become harder to find and much more expensive, and many owners are now combining a California FAIR Plan fire policy with a second policy that covers everything else, to get full coverage at a manageable cost. Buyers should get real insurance quotes during their contingency period, before they're committed to the purchase.**

Quick but important note before we go further: I'm a real estate agent, not an insurance professional. Everything below is general background to help you ask better questions. Your actual options, pricing and coverage depend on your specific property, and a licensed insurance agent or broker is the person who can tell you what makes sense for you.

## What Happened to Insurance in the Foothills

The [Eaton Fire](https://www.fire.ca.gov/incidents/2025/1/7/eaton-fire) started on January 7, 2025 and burned 14,021 acres, destroying 9,419 structures and damaging another 1,076, with 19 lives lost. It hit Altadena and parts of Pasadena hardest, just a few miles east of La Cañada Flintridge and La Crescenta.

Insurance was already getting tight in California's wildfire-prone areas before 2025. After the Eaton and Palisades fires, it got a lot tighter. Many traditional insurers have pulled back on writing new policies or renewing existing ones in high-fire-risk areas, and the ones still writing have raised prices considerably.

[The result shows up in the numbers at the state's insurer of last resort](https://insurancenewsnet.com/oarticle/california-fair-plan-rates-going-up-29-1-in-late-2026). The California FAIR Plan had 464,900 homeowner policies in fall 2024 and 668,600 by the end of 2025, a 44 percent jump in about a year.

## What the California FAIR Plan Actually Is (and Isn't)

[The FAIR Plan](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/200-wrr/California-FAIR-Plan.cfm) is not a state agency and not a government handout. It's an insurance pool backed by the private insurers licensed in California, and it serves as the state's insurer of last resort for people who can't get coverage from a regular insurer.

Here's the part that surprises people: the [standard FAIR Plan dwelling policy](https://www.cfpnet.com/policies/dwelling/) is a named-peril policy covering fire, lightning, internal explosion and smoke. That's basically it. It does not cover things a normal homeowners policy does, like theft, water damage from a burst pipe, or personal liability if someone gets hurt on your property.

[Residential FAIR Plan coverage](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/200-wrr/California-FAIR-Plan.cfm) is capped at $3 million per policyholder. In a market where the median single family home in La Crescenta runs around $1.4 million and La Cañada Flintridge runs well above that, it's worth confirming with your insurance agent or broker that the limit actually covers what it would cost to rebuild your specific home.

## The FAIR Plan + Companion Policy Combo, Explained

Because the FAIR Plan only covers fire and a handful of related perils, it's meant to be paired with a second policy that covers everything else. [The FAIR Plan itself recommends this](https://www.cfpnet.com/policies/dwelling/), suggesting Difference in Conditions (DIC), flood or earthquake policies to fill in the gaps.

That second policy can take a couple of forms:

- **A Difference in Conditions (DIC) policy**, sometimes called a wraparound policy. This is a standalone policy from a private insurer built specifically to cover what the FAIR Plan doesn't.
- **A regular homeowners policy with a FAIR Plan companion endorsement.** This is what I have, and it's not a DIC. It's a standard homeowners policy from a private insurer with an endorsement that carves out fire and the other perils the FAIR Plan covers, in exchange for a lower premium. The FAIR Plan handles fire. The homeowners policy handles things like theft, water damage and liability.

Either way, the idea is the same: two puzzle pieces that together approximate a full homeowners policy. That's the setup that took my premium from just over $18,000 to just under $5,000. Two premiums, two policies, two sets of paperwork, but a much better total.

One thing I learned reading my own endorsement: it excludes every peril the FAIR Plan *offers*, not just the ones I actually bought from the FAIR Plan. Mine carves out fire and smoke, but also things like windstorm, hail, vandalism and vehicle damage, some of which the FAIR Plan only covers if you add them as optional coverage. If you skip those options on the FAIR Plan side, you may have a gap nobody covers. Your insurance agent can walk you through exactly how your two policies line up, and it's worth asking that question directly.

Is it always cheaper? No. Some homes will do better with a single traditional policy, if one is available to them, and the FAIR Plan has its own drawbacks (more on that below). What makes sense depends on your property, your risk profile and what's actually available on your street, which is exactly why an insurance professional who knows this market is so valuable right now.

## The FAIR Plan Rate Increase Coming October 15, 2026

If you're already on the FAIR Plan, or about to be, mark your calendar. [The California Department of Insurance approved an average 29.1 percent FAIR Plan rate increase](https://insurancenewsnet.com/oarticle/california-fair-plan-rates-going-up-29-1-in-late-2026), down from the 35.8 percent originally requested, taking effect for new and renewing policies on or after October 15, 2026.

That's an average. [According to the FAIR Plan](https://insurancenewsnet.com/oarticle/california-fair-plan-rates-going-up-29-1-in-late-2026), the largest part of the increase is tied to the wildfire portion of premiums, so properties at significant wildfire risk will see higher increases, and some lower-risk policyholders may actually see a decrease. Guess which category a lot of the Foothills falls into.

The bigger picture: [California's Sustainable Insurance Strategy](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/180-climate-change/sustainable-insurance-strategy.Cfm) is the state's attempt to coax private insurers back into high-risk areas. Among other changes, it lets insurers use catastrophe modeling in their rates, and in exchange requires participating insurers to commit to writing more policies in designated "distressed" areas, with the goal of shrinking the FAIR Plan over time. Whether and how quickly that brings more affordable options back to our neighborhoods is still playing out. Let's be real: nobody knows yet. It's one more reason to re-shop your coverage at every renewal instead of assuming last year's setup is still your best option.

## What You Can Actually Do to Lower Your Premium

Some of this is out of your control. Some of it isn't.

**Harden your home.** [California's Safer from Wildfires regulation](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/200-wrr/Safer-from-Wildfires.cfm) requires insurers to offer discounts for specific mitigation steps, including a Class A fire-rated roof, ember- and fire-resistant vents, enclosed eaves, upgraded multi-paned windows, a 5-foot ember-resistant zone around the house, a 6-inch non-combustible base on exterior walls, clearing vegetation under decks, and maintaining defensible space. The FAIR Plan also offers [wildfire hardening discounts](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/200-wrr/California-FAIR-Plan.cfm) for residential policyholders. Ask your insurance professional which improvements your specific carriers actually credit, and how much, before you spend money on them.

**Look into your neighborhood.** Community-level programs like Firewise USA can also qualify for discounts under Safer from Wildfires. If your street or HOA isn't part of one yet, it might be worth rallying a few neighbors to start. It's a shared project that can lower premiums for everyone on the block.

**Talk to someone who knows the FAIR Plan setup.** Not every insurance professional regularly structures FAIR Plan combinations, so ask directly whether they do. If your agent represents a single company, it's also worth getting a comparison quote from an independent broker, who can shop several carriers at once. Having someone who knew how to put the pieces together is what made the difference for me.

**Re-shop every renewal.** The market is moving fast. The best option last year may not be the best option this year, in either direction.

## If You're Buying: Get Insurance Quotes Before You're Locked In

This is the part I really want buyers to hear. In the Foothills right now, insurance isn't a box you check the week before closing. It's a real line item that can change how much house you can comfortably afford.

**Get quotes during your contingency period.** Your [investigation contingency](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/what-are-real-estate-contingencies) is your window to find out whether the home is insurable and at what cost, while you still have the ability to back out or renegotiate. Reach out to your insurance agent or broker as soon as you're in contract, or even before you write an offer on a home you're serious about.

**Loop in your lender early.** Your homeowners insurance premium is typically part of the monthly housing payment your [lender looks at when qualifying you](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/pre-approval-vs-pre-qualification), so a bigger premium can affect your numbers. Exactly how it factors in depends on your loan, which is a question for your lender, ideally before you're house hunting in earnest.

**Ask the seller about claims history.** Past insurance claims on a property can affect what it costs to insure going forward. It's reasonable to ask whether the seller has had any claims, and your insurance agent or broker can tell you what information they'll want.

**Ask about hardening that's already been done.** A new Class A roof, ember-resistant vents or enclosed eaves the seller already paid for may help your premium. Those are worth knowing about, and worth documenting.

## If You're Selling: Insurability Is Part of Your Pitch Now

Buyers are asking about insurance earlier and more pointedly than they used to. If you've done mitigation work, have the receipts and details ready. If you know what you're currently paying and how your coverage is structured, that information can help the buyer's insurance agent get quotes faster, which keeps your escrow moving. And if you're weighing whether to make upgrades before listing, fire-hardening improvements are one of the few that may matter to a buyer's monthly budget, not just their first impression.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I still get homeowners insurance in La Crescenta or La Cañada Flintridge?

Generally yes, but it may take more work and cost more than it used to. Many traditional insurers have limited new policies in high-fire-risk areas. Homeowners who can't find traditional coverage can get fire coverage through the California FAIR Plan, often paired with a second policy, such as a Difference in Conditions (DIC) policy or a homeowners policy with a FAIR Plan companion endorsement, for other risks. A licensed insurance professional can tell you what's available for a specific property.

### What does the California FAIR Plan cover?

The standard FAIR Plan dwelling policy is a named-peril policy that covers fire, lightning, internal explosion and smoke. It does not include coverage like theft, water damage or personal liability. Residential FAIR Plan coverage is capped at $3 million per policyholder.

### What policy do you pair with the California FAIR Plan?

Because the FAIR Plan covers only fire and a limited set of related perils, it's typically paired with a second policy from a private insurer. That can be a Difference in Conditions (DIC) policy, a standalone policy built to cover what the FAIR Plan excludes, or a regular homeowners policy with a FAIR Plan companion endorsement that removes fire and other FAIR Plan perils for a lower premium. Together, the two are designed to approximate a standard homeowners policy.

### How much are FAIR Plan rates going up in 2026?

The California Department of Insurance approved an average FAIR Plan rate increase of 29.1 percent, effective for new and renewing policies on or after October 15, 2026. Properties at higher wildfire risk are expected to see larger increases, and some lower-risk policyholders may see decreases.

### When should a homebuyer get insurance quotes in the Foothills?

As early as possible, and no later than the investigation contingency period after your offer is accepted. That gives you time to confirm the home is insurable and understand the cost while you can still renegotiate or cancel. Your lender should also know your expected premium, since it can affect your qualifying numbers.
