"I'm pre-approved." It's one of the first things buyers tell me, and it can mean two very different things depending on who's saying it. Sometimes it means a lender has verified income, assets, and credit and handed over a formal letter with a real number on it. Sometimes it means someone talked to a loan officer once and got a ballpark guess over the phone.

Same sentence. Wildly different meaning. And only one of those means anything when I'm handing your offer to a listing agent.

**Pre-qualification is an informal, self-reported estimate of what you might be able to borrow. Pre-approval is a lender's conditional commitment, backed by verified income, assets, and a credit check, that tells a seller you can actually close. They get used interchangeably in casual conversation, but only one of them means anything when you're competing for a house.**

If you've been using those two words like synonyms, let's fix that before you're standing in front of your dream house with the wrong piece of paper.

## The Actual Difference Between the Two

Pre-qualification is a conversation, not a commitment. You tell a lender what you make, what you owe, and what you've got in the bank, they run those numbers through a quick formula, and you get a ballpark figure. Nobody has verified anything you said. It's genuinely useful as a first gut check on your own budget, but that's about where its usefulness ends.

Pre-approval is a different level of scrutiny entirely. As [CrossCountry Mortgage explains it](https://crosscountrymortgage.com/mortgage/resources/mortgage-pre-approval-vs-pre-qualification/), "the pre-qualification process is usually much simpler and doesn't require any sort of formal application or even a credit check," while pre-approval means you'll "typically need to present tax returns from the past two years, as well as bank statements, pay stubs, and other documentation." What comes out the other end of that process is a real, conditional commitment from an underwriter, not a guess based on what you self-reported.

The credit check matters more than people realize, too. Pre-qualification "doesn't involve a hard credit inquiry" and has no impact on your score, per [CrossCountry Mortgage](https://crosscountrymortgage.com/mortgage/resources/mortgage-pre-approval-vs-pre-qualification/), while "a mortgage pre-approval involves a hard credit inquiry, which typically reduces your credit score," usually only by a handful of points. That's not a reason to skip it. It's a reason to time it intentionally, which we'll get to.

## What You Actually Need to Get Pre-Approved

This is the part that trips people up, mostly because pre-qualification trained them to think a phone call is enough. For a real pre-approval, most lenders are going to ask for:

- Pay stubs covering the last month or so
- W-2s (and often tax returns if you're self-employed or have variable income)
- Bank and investment account statements to verify assets
- Photo ID
- An explanation for anything unusual: a large deposit, an employment gap, a recent job change

None of this is exotic. It's the same paperwork you'll need eventually anyway, you're just handing it over earlier so your offer has teeth when it matters.

One line item worth flagging on its own: if any part of your funds is a gift from family, your lender is going to want a paper trail for that too, typically a signed gift letter confirming it doesn't need to be repaid, plus proof it actually moved from their account to yours. It's common, but it trips up more buyers than you'd expect, enough that it deserves its own full post soon.

## Why Sellers (and Their Agents) Actually Care

This isn't agents being precious about paperwork. It's risk management. A seller who accepts your offer is taking their home off the market, often turning down other interest, on the strength of your ability to close. [Zillow reports that 85% of sellers say they prefer to accept an offer from a buyer who's already pre-approved](https://www.zillow.com/learn/pre-qualification-vs-pre-approval/), and that's before you factor in how much financing has already gotten more unpredictable for both sides.

It also matters who else is in the room. All-cash buyers made up [26% of primary residence purchases nationally in NAR's 2025 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers](https://www.nar.realtor/blogs/economists-outlook/top-10-takeaways-from-nars-2025-profile-of-home-buyers-and-sellers), an all-time high. If you're financing, you're already competing against buyers who don't need a lender's blessing at all. A verified pre-approval is how you close that credibility gap, at least on paper. A pre-qualification doesn't do that. It just tells the seller you did some mental math.

I said something close to this in [my July La Crescenta market update](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/la-crescenta-market-update-july-2026): two-thirds of single family home sales here closed within 30 days of hitting the market over the past year. That's not a market where you get to shop for financing mid-negotiation. By the time you've found the house, you need to already be past this step.

## How Long Does Pre-Approval Actually Last?

This is where it gets a little lender-dependent, so don't assume. [Zillow puts the average pre-approval letter's shelf life at around 45 days](https://www.zillow.com/learn/pre-qualification-vs-pre-approval/), while [CrossCountry Mortgage](https://crosscountrymortgage.com/mortgage/resources/mortgage-pre-approval-vs-pre-qualification/) puts theirs at 30 to 90 days, renewable with updated documents. Pre-qualification, by contrast, doesn't carry a formal expiration date since it was never more than a quick snapshot. Either way, a pre-approval expires. If your house hunt runs longer than expected, which happens constantly, you'll need to refresh it with current pay stubs and statements before it's usable again.

Practical takeaway: don't get pre-approved six months before you plan to seriously start touring homes. Time it to when you're actually going to be writing offers.

## What Can Still Go Wrong After You're Pre-Approved

Getting the letter isn't the finish line. Lenders re-verify things right before closing, and deals can wobble over stuff that seemed harmless in the moment. Between pre-approval and closing, avoid:

- Financing a car, furniture, or anything else on credit
- Opening a new credit card, even for a "free" sign-up bonus
- Changing jobs or switching from salary to commission-based pay
- Making large deposits into your bank account that you can't easily paper-trail (yes, even Venmo from your parents needs a paper trail)
- Co-signing a loan for anyone else, no matter how well you know them

None of these will necessarily sink your loan. But any of them can force a re-underwrite, delay your closing, or in a worse case, change your approval terms entirely. If something financially unusual is coming up, tell your lender before it happens, not after they find it during final verification.

## Getting Pre-Approved by More Than One Lender Won't Tank Your Credit

A lot of buyers avoid shopping lenders because they're worried about stacking up credit inquiries. Reasonable instinct, wrong conclusion. Credit scoring models are built to dedupe mortgage-related inquiries made within a short window: [myFICO notes that older FICO models group them within 14 days, and newer models expand that to 45 days](https://www.myfico.com/credit-education/blog/rate-shop). Since you don't always know which model a given lender is using, the safe move is to get your rate comparisons done within about 14 days if you can. Shop your rate. It's designed to cost you less than people assume.

## How to Actually Get Pre-Approved

1. Pick a lender (or two or three, see above) and ask what they need from you specifically, since requirements vary slightly.
2. Gather your documents: pay stubs, W-2s or tax returns, bank statements, ID.
3. Submit and let them run a full credit check.
4. Get your letter, and actually read it. It should state a specific loan amount, loan type, and expiration date, not just a vague "you're approved."
5. Understand the difference between your max approved amount and what you're actually comfortable paying every month. Those are rarely the same number, and they shouldn't be.

That last point matters more than people give it credit for. Your lender is telling you what you qualify for on paper. Only you know what that payment actually feels like against your real life, your other goals, and your comfort with risk. This is exactly the kind of thing worth talking through with your agent before you start touring, alongside [what your Buyer-Broker Representation Agreement actually covers](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/buyer-broker-representation-agreement-california) and [the other questions worth asking before you hire one](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/questions-first-time-homebuyers-should-ask-real-estate-agent). And if rates are the thing making your approved number feel tight, it's worth knowing [what rate buydowns can actually do to that monthly payment](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/interest-rate-buydown-programs-explained) before you rule out a price range you could otherwise afford.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is mortgage pre-approval free?

Yes, in the vast majority of cases. Lenders don't typically charge for pre-approval itself, since it's part of how they compete for your business. You should be wary of any lender asking for a fee just to issue a pre-approval letter.

### Does getting pre-approved hurt my credit score?

It can cause a small, temporary dip because it usually involves a hard credit pull, but the effect is minor and short-lived. Shopping multiple lenders within a short window (about 14 days, to be safe across different scoring models) is treated as a single inquiry for scoring purposes, not one per lender.

### Can I make an offer with just a pre-qualification letter?

You can, but you're taking a real risk in any competitive market. Since pre-qualification isn't verified, sellers and listing agents weigh it far less than a pre-approval, and in a multiple-offer situation it can be the difference between your offer getting a serious look and getting passed over.

### How soon before house hunting should I get pre-approved?

Time it to when you're actually ready to tour and write offers, not months in advance. Most pre-approval letters expire in 30 to 90 days depending on the lender, so getting one too early just means refreshing it later with updated documents.

## The Bottom Line

Pre-qualification tells you roughly what you might be able to spend. Pre-approval tells a seller you can actually close. If you're serious about buying, especially in a market where two-thirds of homes are selling within 30 days, the second one isn't optional homework, it's the price of admission to actually compete.
