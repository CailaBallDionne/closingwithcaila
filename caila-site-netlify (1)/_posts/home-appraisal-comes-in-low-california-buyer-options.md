Few phrases make people tense up faster in escrow than "the appraisal came in low." Buyers worry about the money. Sellers worry about the sale. And some people even think the appraiser is channeling Tamra Judge (Housewives, obv) yelling "THAT'S MY OPINION!" on a decision affecting a huge financial purchase (Or maybe I'm the only one whose brain defaults to Bravo in times of stress).

Most of that stress around this part of escrow comes from not knowing what an appraisal is, not preparing for what happens when the number lands below the price, and lack of clarity around what your options are afterward. The appraiser is a licensed professional doing a specific job for the lender: estimating what the home is worth, based largely on recent comparable sales. Once you understand that job and the choices that follow, a low appraisal is less of a scary surprise and more of a (often predictable) problem with known solutions.

You may have heard "appraisal gap" mentioned in multiple-offer situations in La Crescenta, La Cañada Flintridge, and the rest of the Foothills. Here is what it means, what happens when one shows up, and how to prepare before you ever have to decide in a hurry.

**An appraisal gap is the difference between the price in your purchase agreement and the lower value the lender's appraiser assigns the home. Because lenders base the loan amount on the appraised value rather than the purchase price, a low appraisal leaves you with four main options: ask for a reconsideration of value, renegotiate the price, cover the gap with your own cash, or cancel the contract if your appraisal contingency is still in place.**

Here is how each one works, and how to avoid being surprised by any of them.

## What an Appraisal Is (and What It Is Not)

When you finance a purchase, your lender hires a licensed appraiser to assess the home's value, because the home is the collateral for the loan. The lender wants to confirm the property is worth what they are being asked to lend against. The appraisal fee is typically part of the buyer's closing costs.

An appraisal is not a home inspection. The inspector tells you what condition the house is in. The appraiser gives an opinion of what it is worth, based largely on how it compares to similar homes that have recently sold nearby.

An appraisal that comes below contract price is not entirely rare. One lender's data, [cited by Homes.com](https://www.homes.com/learn/low-appraisal/), put the share of appraisals that came in below the contract price at about 8.6 percent in early 2026. That is a single lender's figure, not a market-wide number, but it is a good reminder that this is something to plan for rather than something that only happens to other people.

## Why a Low Appraisal Changes Your Math

This is the part that surprises people. A low appraisal changes how much cash you need.

Here is a simple hypothetical, because I truly love math. Your actual loan terms depend on your loan program and your lender, so treat this as an illustration of the idea and not a prediction.
- Purchase price in your contract: $1,000,000
- Planned down payment: 20 percent, or $200,000
- Planned loan: $800,000
- The home appraises at $960,000, a $40,000 gap

If the loan is sized at 80 percent of the appraised value instead of the price, the loan drops to $768,000. The contract price has not changed, so the difference has to come from somewhere. Your cash to close rises from $200,000 to $232,000, which is $32,000 more than you planned for.

That is why the first call after a low appraisal should be to your lender, to confirm exactly how your loan changes. Your loan officer can run the real numbers for your program.

## Why Appraisals Come in Low

There is no single cause, but a few reasons come up often:
- **Competition outpaced the comps.** Appraisers generally lean on recently closed sales. When a listing draws multiple offers and the winning price lands above what recent sales support, the appraisal can land below the contract.
- **Few comparable sales.** A home with an unusual layout, a large lot, a view, or a one-of-a-kind remodel can be hard to compare, and the appraiser has to work with what is available.
- **A fast-moving market.** If prices are moving quickly in either direction, the most recent closed sales may not fully reflect where buyers are actually willing to pay today.
- **Errors in the report.** Wrong square footage, missing upgrades, or comparable sales that are not really comparable can all pull a value down. This is the case where a challenge has the best chance of working.

A low appraisal does not automatically mean you overpaid, and it does not automatically mean the appraiser got it wrong. It means one licensed professional, working from available data, landed on a different number than you and the seller did.

## Your Options if the Appraisal Comes in Low

### 1. Request a Reconsideration of Value

A reconsideration of value, usually called an ROV, is a formal request to review the appraisal. If you or your agent believe there are errors in the report or stronger comparable sales the appraiser did not use, you can submit them. The request is typically submitted through your lender, not directly to the appraiser.

Your agent can help gather the best comparable sales to support the request, and your lender decides how it gets submitted. Be realistic about it, though. Even after an ROV, there is no guarantee the appraiser will change the value, and the process takes time, which matters because your [contingency deadlines](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/what-are-real-estate-contingencies) keep running while you wait.

### 2. Renegotiate the Purchase Price

You can ask the seller to lower the price to the appraised value, or to meet somewhere in the middle. Whether a seller says yes depends on their situation, how the offer was structured, and how many other buyers would be waiting if the deal fell apart. Your leverage is strongest when your appraisal contingency is still active, because the seller knows you have the right to cancel.

### 3. Cover the Gap in Cash

You can also keep the original price and bring the difference to closing yourself. This is the option that most often keeps a deal intact, and it is also the one that requires the most honesty with yourself about your finances.

Before you commit to it, ask your lender how the gap affects your loan and your cash to close, and make sure you would still have a healthy cash cushion after closing. If family is helping with your down payment, ask your lender whether any additional gift funds can be used for this purpose and how they need to be documented. My post on [gift funds and gift letters](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/down-payment-gift-funds-california) covers the basics.

### 4. Cancel Under Your Appraisal Contingency

If your contract includes an appraisal contingency and you have not removed it, you can cancel and recover your deposit if the home does not appraise at the agreed price and you and the seller cannot work something out. My guide to [real estate contingencies](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/what-are-real-estate-contingencies) explains how this protection works alongside the others.

Timing matters here. In the standard C.A.R. residential purchase agreement, contingencies must be removed in writing, and the default removal period for the appraisal contingency is [17 days after acceptance](https://www.car.org/-/media/CAR/Documents/Transaction-Center/PDF/Standard-Forms/RPA_12-2022.pdf), unless the contract says otherwise. The same C.A.R. guide notes that the contingencies do not simply disappear on day 17, but once that period passes, the seller can deliver a Notice to Buyer to Perform, which starts a short clock for you to remove the contingency or cancel. Your agent should be tracking the appraisal timeline against these dates from day one.

### What If You Already Removed the Appraisal Contingency?

Then the picture changes. If the appraisal contingency has been removed or waived and the home appraises low, you may be expected to cover the gap in cash or risk losing your deposit. This is one of the real risks of waiving the appraisal contingency, and it is worth a direct conversation with your agent before you sign off on removing it.

## Appraisal Gap Coverage in Your Offer

In competitive situations, some buyers build the gap into the offer itself. An appraisal gap clause is a promise that if the home appraises below the price, the buyer will cover the shortfall in cash, up to a stated amount.

The C.A.R. purchase agreement has a built-in way to do this. The appraisal line lets the contingency be based on an appraised value at a minimum of the purchase price or a different dollar amount that you specify. Setting that number below the price is, in practice, a way of saying you are willing to cover that much of a gap.

This can make an offer more attractive without a full waiver, but it is a commitment of real dollars. The cap should reflect cash you could actually bring to the table without stretching, not the number that sounds most competitive. Talk through it with your lender before you write it into an offer.

## What About FHA, VA, and Other Loan Programs?

Appraisal rules and what happens after a low value can vary by loan program, and that is lender territory, so your loan officer is the right person to ask. As one example, with a VA loan, the VA guarantee is based on the lower appraised value if the appraisal comes in under the price. Ask your lender early how a low appraisal would play out for your specific program, before you are in the middle of one.

## If You Are the Seller

A low appraisal does not automatically end a sale. Sellers generally have similar levers: they can ask for a reconsideration of value, agree to adjust the price, hold the price if the buyer is willing to bring the difference in cash, or let the deal end and go back on the market. Which one makes sense depends on the strength of the buyer, the contract, and how the home has been received so far.

## How to Reduce the Odds of an Appraisal Surprise

You cannot control the appraiser, but you can control some of what leads up to it:
- **Look at the comps before you offer.** Ask your agent to walk you through the recent closed sales that support the price, and where the numbers could get tight.
- **Know your cash cushion.** Decide in advance how much of a gap you could cover comfortably, so you are not making that call under pressure.
- **Get fully pre-approved.** A strong pre-approval gives you a clearer picture of your loan, and my post on [pre-approval versus pre-qualification](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/pre-approval-vs-pre-qualification) explains why the difference matters.
- **Track your deadlines.** My breakdown of [what happens between an accepted offer and closing](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/what-happens-after-offer-accepted-california-escrow-timeline) shows where the appraisal fits into the escrow timeline.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is an appraisal gap?

An appraisal gap is the difference between the agreed purchase price and the lower value the lender's appraiser assigns to the home. Because lenders generally base the loan amount on the appraised value, the gap often has to be handled through a price reduction, extra cash from the buyer, or a successful challenge to the appraisal.

### Can I back out if a home appraises low in California?

If your purchase agreement includes an appraisal contingency and you have not removed it, you generally have the right to cancel and get your deposit back if the home does not appraise at the agreed price and you cannot reach a different agreement with the seller. If you have already removed the contingency, canceling could put your deposit at risk, so check with your agent before taking any step.

### Does a low appraisal mean I overpaid?

Not necessarily. An appraisal is one professional's opinion of value, based largely on recent comparable sales. It can come in lower than the contract price because of limited comps, a fast-moving market, or errors in the report, and it is possible to request a reconsideration of value if you believe there are errors or better comparable sales.

### Who pays for the appraisal?

The appraisal fee is typically included in the buyer's closing costs. Your lender can tell you the exact amount for your loan.

## The Bottom Line

A low appraisal is stressful, but it is rarely the end of the road. It is a decision point, and the buyers who handle it best are the ones who understood their options and their numbers before it happened.
