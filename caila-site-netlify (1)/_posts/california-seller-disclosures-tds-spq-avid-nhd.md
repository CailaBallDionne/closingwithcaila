Good news: I have yet to see seller disclosures cause anyone to throw a drink. Better news: Once you know what each form is for, most of it comes down to telling the truth, in writing, about a house you already know well. And most buyers just want to know what they're buying into so there's no post-closing surprises.

So let's dig into it.

**California home sellers typically deliver four disclosures to buyers: the Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS) and the Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ), which the seller completes; the Agent Visual Inspection Disclosure (AVID), which the agents complete; and the Natural Hazard Disclosure (NHD), which is based on a third-party report on whether the property sits in a mapped hazard zone.**

## The short answer

California law puts the burden of disclosure on the seller and on the agents involved. The idea is simple: a buyer should know what you know, and what a reasonably careful agent can see, before they commit to the biggest purchase of their life.

Here is the quick version of who does what:

- **TDS:** completed by the seller, and the agent has a section too.

- **SPQ:** completed by the seller, with more detailed questions than the TDS.

- **AVID:** completed by the listing agent, and separately by the buyer's agent.

- **NHD:** prepared from a third-party report, then signed off by the seller and agent.

If you want the longer version, keep reading.

## The Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS)

This is the main event. [California Civil Code Section 1102](https://homelight.com/blog/transfer-disclosure-statement) requires sellers of most residential properties to complete this form, and it walks through the property's features, any known malfunctions or defects, and a set of questions about the history of the home.

Most of those questions are phrased as "are you aware of...?" Things like room additions or structural changes, past flooding or drainage problems, major damage from events like fire or earthquakes, and issues with neighbors or the neighborhood. When the answer is yes, you explain.

One detail worth knowing: the California Association of REALTORS® notes that the TDS and its companion forms are [written in very broad language on purpose](https://cdn.realtyfeed.com/cdn/3/1177106473/852f68ee4fbdd7e3e0ffec0187b93dc5.pdf), designed to get sellers to give buyers as much information as possible. If you are on the fence about whether something is worth mentioning, that design is a good hint about which way the form wants you to lean.

California law also says [each disclosure must be made in good faith](https://californiaglobe.com/articles/residential-property-transfer-disclosures/). That is the whole philosophy in one line.

## The Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ)

Think of the SPQ as the TDS with the follow-up questions already asked. It is another form from the California Association of REALTORS® that sellers [may be required to complete alongside the TDS](https://cdn.realtyfeed.com/cdn/3/1177106473/852f68ee4fbdd7e3e0ffec0187b93dc5.pdf), and it digs into the property's history and condition in more detail.

Your listing agent will tell you which forms apply to your situation. Some sellers, such as certain trustees, are exempt from completing the TDS and use a different form called the Exempt Seller Disclosure (ESD). If you are selling as a trustee or on behalf of an estate, this [seller advisory](https://files.constantcontact.com/58608e8b001/e6773705-3ac6-479e-9d83-b77c2c538f00.pdf) is a good place to start, and it is also worth a conversation with an attorney.

## The Agent Visual Inspection Disclosure (AVID)

The AVID is where the agent has homework. Under [Civil Code Section 2079](https://media.crmls.org/medias/6f31edba-2588-4447-a5c9-0e69d276a54e.pdf), California requires agents, with limited exceptions, to conduct a reasonably competent and diligent visual inspection of the reasonably and normally accessible areas of certain properties, then disclose the material facts that inspection reveals.

Two things to understand about it:

- **It is a visual walk-through, not a home inspection.** The form itself says the agent's inspection is not intended to replace any other type of inspection. Your agent is not crawling the attic with a flashlight and a moisture meter.

- **Both sides do one.** The listing agent completes an AVID, and the buyer's agent completes their own based on their own visit.

If your listing agent is walking around your home taking notes before you list, this is why. It is not judgment. It is a legal requirement.

## The Natural Hazard Disclosure (NHD)

The NHD answers a different question: where does the property sit on the map? Under [Civil Code Section 1103](https://legaltemplates.net/wp-content/uploads/California-Natural-Hazard-Disclosure-Statement.pdf), the statement covers six hazard zones:

1. A special flood hazard area

2. An area of potential flooding from dam failure

3. A very high fire hazard severity zone

4. A wildland fire area (state fire responsibility area)

5. An earthquake fault zone

6. A seismic hazard zone

You do not research this yourself. The statement is typically prepared from a report by an independent third-party provider, and the form includes an acknowledgment that [the seller and agent used good faith in selecting that provider](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/civil-code/civ-sect-1103-2/). The same section also states that the NHD does not constitute all of the seller's or agent's disclosure obligations. It is one piece of the packet, not a substitute for the rest.

Here in the Foothills, fire hazard severity zones are a big enough part of the local conversation that it is worth reading your own NHD carefully before a buyer does. Buyers here are also thinking about [homeowners insurance in the Foothills](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/foothills-homeowners-insurance-after-eaton-fire), so the more you know about your property's zones, the better prepared you are for those questions. Surprises are better when they are in your hands first.

## What changed in 2026

California adds to the disclosure rules fairly often, and 2026 was no exception. According to the [San Francisco Association of REALTORS®](https://my.sfrealtors.com/2026/02/09/immediate-release-of-revised-san-francisco-seller-disclosure-all-properties-sfsd-all/), three new subsections of Civil Code Section 1102.6 took effect January 1, 2026:

- **Electrical systems:** a required statement advising buyers to inspect the electrical system.

- **Gas appliance replacement rules:** disclosure of the seller's or agent's awareness of state or local requirements or restrictions on future replacement of gas-powered appliances.

- **Smoke and nicotine residue:** disclosure of actual knowledge of smoke or nicotine residue or historic smoking on the property. [Law firm summaries](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/landlord-tenant-leases/1743180/2026-california-real-estate-law-highlights-every-property-owner-should-know) refer to this as the "thirdhand smoke" disclosure.

If you are selling a condo or a home in an HOA (and want the buyer's view, here is [how to read an HOA's budget, reserve study, and assessment history](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/buying-a-condo-los-angeles-hoa-guide)), there is a separate change. [SB 410](https://baylegal.com/hoa-disclosure-issues-in-california-real-estate-what-buyers-and-sellers-must-know/) added the most recent exterior elevated element inspection report (the balcony and deck inspections under SB 326) to the HOA document package that sellers deliver under Civil Code Section 4525.

Your listing agent and the current CAR forms will reflect these updates. I mention them mostly so that none of it is a surprise when you see a form that looks different from the one your neighbor filled out three years ago.

## When does the buyer get all of this?

Under [Civil Code Section 1102.3](https://california.public.law/codes/civil_code_section_1102.3), the TDS has to be delivered as soon as practicable before transfer of title. If a required disclosure, or a material amendment to one, is delivered after the buyer's offer has been made or accepted, the buyer gets three days after in-person delivery, or five days after mail or agreed electronic delivery, to cancel the offer by written notice.

That cancellation window is a good reason to get the paperwork done early. A common approach is to put the full disclosure package in front of buyers before offers are written, so everyone is making decisions with the same information and nobody is renegotiating from a surprise. Buyers can then use their [investigation contingency](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/what-are-real-estate-contingencies) to dig into anything on the forms that raises a question.

## Does selling as-is get you out of disclosures?

No, sorry. Selling as-is is a conversation about repairs: you are telling the buyer you will not be fixing things. It is not a pass on telling them what you know.

Even sellers who are exempt from completing certain forms are still [expected to disclose known facts that materially affect the value and desirability of the property](https://my.sfrealtors.com/2026/02/09/immediate-release-of-revised-san-francisco-seller-disclosure-all-properties-sfsd-all/). Because the details of an as-is sale can get legal quickly, a California real estate attorney is the right person to ask about your specific situation.

## A few things I would do before filling anything out

I am not an attorney, and none of this is legal advice. But the general guidance from California's seller disclosure advisories is consistent, and it is practical:

1. **Gather what you already have.** The [CAR seller advisory](https://cdn.realtyfeed.com/cdn/3/1177106473/852f68ee4fbdd7e3e0ffec0187b93dc5.pdf) suggests locating prior inspection reports, repair estimates, invoices, plans, and permits, and using them to answer the questions. Prior reports are typically provided along with the forms.

2. **Start early.** The advisory also says to allow plenty of time. These forms are long, and rushing is how a question gets skipped.

3. **Answer for the house you actually have.** You are not expected to discover problems you do not know about, but you are expected to be accurate about the ones you do. That includes work done without permits. If that is on your radar, my post on [what to do if your ADU is not permitted](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/should-you-build-adu-before-selling-california) walks through the options.

4. **Update if something changes.** Seller advisories commonly recommend [correcting and updating your disclosures in a timely way](https://files.constantcontact.com/58608e8b001/e6773705-3ac6-479e-9d83-b77c2c538f00.pdf) through the close of escrow.

5. **Remember a death on the property.** [California law also addresses deaths that occurred on the property within the past three years](https://homelight.com/blog/transfer-disclosure-statement). Ask your agent or attorney how it applies to you.

One more honest note: if you order a pre-listing inspection, you will know more about your house afterward, and that knowledge generally goes into your disclosures. Many sellers still find that worth it because there are fewer surprises later, but it is a decision to make with your agent.

## FAQ

**Do I still have to complete disclosures if I sell my house as-is?**
Selling as-is generally changes who pays for repairs, not whether you disclose what you know. The state's disclosure forms are built around what the seller is aware of, and the forms are written to get sellers to share as much information as possible. If you are unsure how an as-is sale affects your obligations, a California real estate attorney is the right person to ask.

**What if I don't know about a problem with the house?**
A seller is not generally expected to investigate or discover unknown issues. The disclosure questions ask what you are aware of. That said, if you have prior inspection reports, repair invoices, or permits, those should be gathered and used to answer the questions accurately, and copies are typically provided along with the forms.

**Who fills out which disclosure form?**
The seller completes the Transfer Disclosure Statement and, depending on the forms used in the transaction, the Seller Property Questionnaire. The listing agent and the buyer's agent each complete an Agent Visual Inspection Disclosure based on their own walk-through. The Natural Hazard Disclosure is generally prepared from a report by a third-party provider that checks the property against state and federal hazard maps.

**Can a buyer cancel after receiving the disclosures?**
If a required disclosure or a material amendment to one is delivered after the buyer's offer has been made or accepted, California law gives the buyer three days after in-person delivery, or five days after delivery by mail or agreed electronic delivery, to terminate the offer in writing. Delivering disclosures before offers come in is one way to avoid that window.

**What if something changes after I've submitted my disclosures?**
Seller advisories commonly recommend updating and correcting your disclosure forms in a timely way through the close of escrow if something changes or you learn something new. Talk to your agent right away when that happens so the update is handled properly.

## The bottom line

Disclosures are not a dinner party game. Nobody is trying to catch you in a lie, and nobody is flipping a table. They are a structured, in-writing way of being straightforward with the person about to buy your home, and the goal is simple: no surprises after closing. Save the drama for the reunion special.

Fill them out carefully, lean toward sharing, get help from your agent and an attorney when something is unclear, and update them if anything changes.
