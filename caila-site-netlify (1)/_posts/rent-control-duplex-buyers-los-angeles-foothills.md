Duplexes, triplexes, and fourplexes are one of the more approachable ways into both homeownership and investing in Los Angeles. Some buyers live in one unit and rent out the rest. Others buy the whole building as an investment. It is a smart strategy, and it comes with a catch you don't get when you buy a single family home: the rent rules that apply depend on what year the building was built, whether you plan to live in it, and which side of a city line it sits on.

Before anyone buys a rental property, someone usually builds a spreadsheet projecting how much rent the building brings in and what's left after expenses. (Agents and investors call it a pro forma.) These spreadsheets tend to paint a beautiful and profitable picture, because they assume you can charge the rent on the page. The picture gets a lot less pretty when you learn that the rent in the spreadsheet isn't a rent you're legally allowed to charge.

**When you buy a rental property in California, you take on the rent rules that come with it. Many rentals, especially multi-unit buildings that are more than 15 years old, are covered by AB 1482, the state law that limits annual rent increases to 5 percent plus inflation (10 percent at most) and requires a legal reason to end a tenancy after 12 months. Where a stricter local ordinance exists, it takes over. In the Foothills area that can mean Los Angeles County's rules in unincorporated La Crescenta and Montrose, the City of Los Angeles's RSO, or Pasadena's Measure H. Before you write an offer, find out which rules cover the building, what the tenants pay today, and whether the seller has followed the rules so far.**

Quick but important note: I'm a real estate agent, not an attorney. This is general background to help you ask better questions, and these rules get amended often. A landlord-tenant attorney, along with the city or county agency that administers the rules, can tell you how they apply to a specific property.

## You're Buying the Rent Roll, Not Just the Building

When you buy a building with tenants, you also buy their leases, their current rents, and the legal limits on how those rents can change. The rent in the listing is the rent today. It is not necessarily what the unit would fetch on the open market, and it is not a number you can adjust on closing day.

Here is why the details matter. Say a unit rents for $2,500 a month. Under a 3 percent local cap, the most you could raise it in a year is $75, to $2,575. Under a state cap of 8 percent, the same increase could be $200. Same unit, same tenant, a $125 difference every month, or $1,500 a year, depending on which rule governs. (Those are illustrative numbers, not a forecast, and the caps change.)

So if the first question on any small multifamily property is "what's the rent?" the second is "which rules apply to this rent?"

## Layer One: AB 1482, the Statewide Rules

California's Tenant Protection Act of 2019, known as AB 1482, is the baseline. It has two parts:

- **A rent cap.** Under [Civil Code Section 1947.12](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1947.12), when a tenant stays, an owner can't raise the rent over any 12-month period by more than 5 percent plus the change in the regional consumer price index, or 10 percent, whichever is lower. The increase is measured against the lowest rent charged for the unit in the prior 12 months, and there can be no more than two increases in that window. The cap limits increases for tenants who stay. When a new tenancy begins and no tenant from the prior one remains, the owner can set the starting rent. In the Los Angeles area, the cap has recently worked out to roughly [8 percent](https://www.saje.net/?p=14448) to [8.7 percent](https://www.steadily.com/blog/how-much-can-a-landlord-raise-rent-in-california), depending on the effective date, and it is recalculated every year.

- **Just cause for eviction.** Under [Civil Code Section 1946.2](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1946.2), once a tenant has lived in a unit for 12 months, the owner needs a legally recognized reason to end the tenancy. Before that point, state law doesn't require a reason, though the owner still has to follow the notice rules and any lease terms. Local ordinances often start protecting tenants sooner. [LA's Just Cause Ordinance, for example, applies at the end of the first lease or six months after move-in, whichever comes first](https://cd9.lacity.gov/articles/just-cause-tenant-eviction-protections). We'll get into what that means for owner move-ins and renovations in a minute.

**What AB 1482 doesn't cover.** The statute lists several exemptions. The ones most relevant to buyers:

- **Newer buildings.** Housing with a certificate of occupancy issued within the previous 15 years is exempt from the cap. That window rolls forward every year.

- **Single family homes and condos, with conditions.** These are exempt from the cap if the owner isn't a real estate investment trust, a corporation, or an LLC with a corporate member, and the tenants were given the specific written exemption notice the statute requires.

- **Some owner-occupied duplexes.** A duplex is exempt if the two units are in a single structure, the owner lived in one as their principal residence when the tenancy began and still does, and neither unit is an accessory dwelling unit (ADU) or junior ADU.

- **Housing already under stricter local rent control.** If a local ordinance limits annual increases to less than the state formula, the local rule governs instead.

**One more thing worth knowing:** AB 1482's rent cap and its just cause rules, found in [Civil Code Section 1947.12](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1947.12) and [Civil Code Section 1946.2](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1946.2), are both written to be repealed on January 1, 2030, unless the Legislature extends them. Local ordinances are separate laws, so they don't expire with it.

## Layer Two: Local Rules, Which Usually Matter More

In the Foothills and the neighborhoods around it, the local ordinance often sets the number that matters, because it is stricter than the state's. A few that come up here:

- **Unincorporated La Crescenta and Montrose (Los Angeles County).** This one surprises people: [La Crescenta-Montrose is unincorporated](https://www.laalmanac.com/cities/ci95u16.php), so there's no city hall, and the County's rules apply instead. The County's rent control covers [buildings with two or more rental units that have a certificate of occupancy dated February 1, 1995 or earlier](https://dcba.lacounty.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Rent-Increase-Bulletin-RSTPO-June2026-July2027.pdf). (A certificate of occupancy is the document showing a building was approved for people to live in.) It applies even if a unit was never permitted.

  Rent can go up once a year by 60 percent of inflation, with a 3 percent maximum. From July 1, 2026 through June 30, 2027, that works out to 1.919 percent. Owners who qualify as "small property landlords" can add 1 more percentage point. That group includes owners who get the homeowners' property tax exemption, which may describe someone living in one of the units.

  Before a landlord can raise the rent at all, they have to register each unit with the County, be current on registration fees, and give tenants the County's notice of tenant rights.

- **City of Los Angeles (RSO).** This applies only to buildings inside the City of Los Angeles itself, which in your search area mostly means Northeast LA neighborhoods like Eagle Rock and Highland Park, not La Crescenta, La Cañada Flintridge, Glendale, or Pasadena. [The RSO generally covers buildings with two or more units that were built before October 1978](https://www.saje.net/?p=14448). [The annual increase is 3 percent through June 30, 2027](https://cd4.lacity.gov/initiative/renters-rights/), and as of February 2026 landlords can no longer add extra percentage points for utilities. Units have to be registered with the Los Angeles Housing Department every year. [LAHD's overview lists duplexes among the covered property types](https://housing.lacity.gov/residents/renters/rso-overview), so don't assume that living in one unit gets you out of it. LAHD also notes that the City's Just Cause Ordinance applies even to rentals the RSO doesn't cover.

- **Pasadena (Measure H).** [Measure H covers multi-unit buildings built before February 1, 1995](https://www.cityofpasadena.net/rent-stabilization/rent-stabilization-overview/), but not condos or single family homes. Landlords can raise rent once every 12 months with 30 days' written notice, up to an annual figure set by the city's Rental Housing Board. The City [announced 2.5 percent](https://www.cityofpasadena.net/rent-stabilization/news-announcements/pasadena-rental-housing-board-approves-2-5-annual-general-adjustment-for-2026-2027/) for October 1, 2026 through September 30, 2027, and the board sets a new figure each year, so confirm the current one with the city before relying on it.

- **Glendale.** [Glendale doesn't have its own hard cap, so AB 1482 is the limit](https://glendaleca.gov/government/departments/community-development/housing/rent/relocation). It does have a local Rental Rights Program with its own just cause and relocation rules, and [a Tenants Together summary notes](https://tenantstogether.org/?p=671) that in certain older buildings with three or more units, an increase of 7 percent or more can trigger relocation assistance if the tenant moves out because of it.

- **La Cañada Flintridge.** La Cañada Flintridge is its own incorporated city, so the County's rules for unincorporated areas don't apply here. The city's [online municipal code](https://ecode360.com/LA4950) doesn't appear to include a rent control or tenant protection ordinance, which would make the statewide AB 1482 rules the baseline for most rentals. Confirm with the city before you rely on that for a specific property.

- **Other cities add their own twists.** Burbank, for example, [requires relocation assistance of three months' rent](https://www.burbankca.gov/documents/d/community-development/landlord-tenant-faqs-burbank-tenant-protection-ordinance-2025-pdf) for most no-fault terminations.

The point isn't to memorize any of this. It's that two buildings a few blocks apart can follow completely different rules, so the address is the first thing to check.

## "Can I Move In?" and "Can I Renovate?" Are Harder Questions Than They Sound

Many buyers look at a tenant-occupied duplex and think about moving into one unit or renovating it. Both are possible, but the tenant has legal protections, and the rules for ending a tenancy are stricter than they used to be.

Under AB 1482, once a tenant has lived in a unit for 12 months, ending the tenancy for something other than the tenant's fault is called a no-fault just cause termination. Owner move-in and substantial remodel are on that list. At the state level, [no-fault terminations require relocation assistance equal to one month's rent](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1946.2). The owner can pay that amount to the tenant or waive the tenant's last month of rent, and it has to happen within 15 days of the notice.

SB 567, which took effect April 1, 2024, tightened the rules. As I read it, an owner move-in has to be genuine and has to last, and a "substantial remodel" has to be substantial, with permits. Local ordinances can add more, such as higher relocation payments.

If your plan involves living in one of the units, put that on the table early. Ask whether the unit will be vacant at closing, and talk to your lender about occupancy timing for an owner-occupant loan before you write an offer. Your lender is the right person for the loan side of that question. My post on [multifamily financing basics](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/multifamily-financing-basics) covers how owner-occupied financing on two-to-four unit properties generally works.

A related wrinkle for Foothills buyers: the owner-occupied duplex exemption excludes ADUs and junior ADUs. A house with an ADU in the back isn't a duplex for this purpose, and ADUs are treated differently depending on how old they are and where they sit. If an ADU is part of the picture, I wrote about the other side of that decision in [should you build an ADU before selling](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/should-you-build-adu-before-selling-california).

## What I'd Want to Know Before Writing an Offer

You won't have every answer before you're in contract, but you can learn a lot from the listing agent and the seller's disclosures, and you can finish the job during your [investigation contingency](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/what-are-real-estate-contingencies). Before writing an offer on a small multifamily property, I'd want to know:

- **Which rules cover the building.** The year of the certificate of occupancy, the exact address and jurisdiction, and whether the seller is relying on any exemption.

- **Current rents and move-in dates for every unit,** and whether the rents are at, below, or above market. A signed lease and a rent roll are not the same as a rent history.

- **The rent increase history,** including the notices that were served. If anything was raised past the allowed limit, [the statute makes the owner liable for the excess, and for up to three times that amount if the overcharge was willful](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1947.12). The statute's definition of "owner" also includes a predecessor in interest, so if the rent history looks off, ask an attorney what that could mean for you.

- **Proof of registration and fees paid,** if the building is covered by a registry, such as the County's or the City of Los Angeles's. In some places, unregistered units can't legally have rent raised.

- **The exemption notice,** if the seller says a single family home or condo is exempt from AB 1482.

- **Security deposits.** These generally need to be accounted for at closing, so find out how much is held for each unit.

- **Whether the tenants have signed estoppel certificates,** which are statements confirming the rent, the deposit, and the lease terms. Ask your agent and attorney whether that fits your deal.

- **Any unpermitted units or tenant disputes.** Under the County's rules, for instance, units can be covered [regardless of their legal permit status](https://dcba.lacounty.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Rent-Increase-Bulletin-RSTPO-June2026-July2027.pdf).

- **How your lender will count the rent,** and what it means for your qualifying numbers. That one belongs to your lender, and it's worth asking early. If you haven't started that conversation, my post on [pre-approval versus pre-qualification](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/pre-approval-vs-pre-qualification) is a good place to begin.

## Is Rent Control a Dealbreaker?

No, but it is a pricing input. A rent-controlled building can come with long-term tenants, steady income, and fewer vacancies, which is exactly what some buyers are looking for. The tradeoff is that rents can only move so fast, so the price you pay and the rent you can reasonably count on need to make sense together.

Your costs also won't follow the rent cap. Your property taxes [generally reset to what you pay](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/prop-13-lock-in-effect-selling-california) when the property changes hands, and [insurance in the Foothills has gotten harder to find and more expensive](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/foothills-homeowners-insurance-after-eaton-fire). Run the numbers with the rent the rules allow, not the rent you wish the rules allowed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does rent control apply if I buy a duplex and live in one of the units?

It depends on where the building is. Under California's AB 1482, a duplex is exempt from the statewide rent cap and just cause rules if you lived in one of the units as your principal residence when the tenancy began, you continue to live there, and neither unit is an accessory dwelling unit. Local ordinances are separate, and some do not exempt owner-occupied duplexes, so confirm how the city or county where the building sits treats it before you count on an exemption.

### How much can I raise the rent on a duplex or small multifamily building in Los Angeles?

It depends on which rules cover the building. Under AB 1482, the limit is 5 percent plus the change in the regional cost of living, or 10 percent, whichever is lower. Stricter local rules replace that cap where they apply. For example, the City of Los Angeles currently limits increases on rent-stabilized units to 3 percent through June 30, 2027, and Los Angeles County's rules for unincorporated areas limit most covered units to 1.919 percent from July 1, 2026 through June 30, 2027.

### Does rent control apply in La Crescenta and Montrose?

La Crescenta and Montrose are unincorporated, so Los Angeles County's Rent Stabilization and Tenant Protections Ordinance applies rather than a city ordinance. It generally covers rental units on properties with two or more rental units and a certificate of occupancy issued on or before February 1, 1995. Newer buildings may fall under the statewide AB 1482 rules instead.

### If I buy a building with tenants in it, can I raise the rent right away?

Not freely. The tenants' leases and current rents come with the property, and any increase has to follow the state and local limits that apply, including notice rules and, in some jurisdictions, registration requirements. At the state level, an owner can set a new starting rent only when a new tenancy begins and no tenant from the prior tenancy remains in the unit.

### Can I move into a unit that is currently rented if I buy the building?

Not on your own timeline. Once a tenant has lived in a covered unit for 12 months, California generally requires a legal reason to end the tenancy. Owner move-in is one of the allowed no-fault reasons, but at the state level it comes with relocation assistance equal to one month's rent, and SB 567 added rules in 2024 requiring the move-in to be genuine and to last. Local ordinances can add more. If you plan to use an owner-occupant loan, talk with your lender about occupancy timing before you write an offer.

### Does AB 1482 apply to single-family homes and condos?

Generally not, as long as the owner is not a real estate investment trust, a corporation, or an LLC with a corporate member, and the tenants received the written exemption notice that the law requires. Local just cause ordinances may still apply to those rentals, so check the city or county.

### When does AB 1482 expire?

As written, the rent cap and just cause provisions are repealed on January 1, 2030, unless the Legislature extends them. Local ordinances are separate laws and are not tied to that date.

## The Bottom Line

A duplex or small multifamily building can be a great way to build equity in Los Angeles, including in the Foothills. The buyers who do well with them tend to be the ones who learn the rent rules first and fall in love with the building second. Find out which rules cover the property, understand what the tenants pay and how that can change, get the paperwork from the seller, and let an attorney weigh in when the details get complicated.
