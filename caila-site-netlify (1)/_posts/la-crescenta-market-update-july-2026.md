Okay, get comfy. We're about to go deep into the La Crescenta housing market. If you love data as much as I do, this is your beach read. (If you prefer the pretty photos side of real estate, I gotchu.)

I pulled a full twelve months of MLS data, August 2025 through July 2026, for La Crescenta (which for MLS purposes bundles in Glendale's Montrose and La Crescenta-Montrose annex areas), and lined it up against the same twelve months a year earlier. Then I did the same for La Cañada Flintridge, because the two markets get compared constantly. Then, because I got curious and you should always follow the data where it leads, I pulled the rental market too. Single family homes, condos, leases, both cities. This thorough look is going to help those who are seriously considering buying or selling here and want more than a "the market is hot" Instagram caption.

**La Crescenta's single family home market held remarkably steady on price over the past year (median around $1.4 million, essentially flat) while sales activity and buyer urgency both picked up meaningfully, months of supply tightened from four months to two, and the condo market saw a genuine surge in both inventory and closings.**

That's the headline. Here's everything underneath it.

## The Big Picture: La Crescenta Single Family Homes, Year Over Year

Comparing July 2026 to July 2025 directly (our most recent full month against the same month a year prior), a few things stand out:

Closed sales were up meaningfully: 21 single family homes closed in La Crescenta in July 2025, compared to 26 in July 2026, a 24 percent increase. Pending sales, which tell you what's coming down the pipe, jumped even more, up from 22 in July 2025 to 37 in July 2026, a 68 percent increase year over year. That's not a small move. That's a market where more buyers are actively writing offers right now than they were twelve months ago.

Months of supply, the cleanest single number for "is this a buyer's or seller's market," tightened from around four months in July 2025 to about two months in July 2026. Anything under three months typically favors sellers. We went from balanced-leaning-buyer to solidly seller-favorable over the course of a year, without prices spiking to match.

And that's the part I want to sit with for a second, because it's a little counterintuitive: median sale price actually moved up only modestly year over year, landing right around $1.4 to $1.55 million depending on the exact week you're measuring, roughly a high single digit percentage increase. Given how much tighter the market got and how much faster buyers were moving, you might expect a bigger price jump. We didn't get one. La Crescenta appreciated, but it didn't spike.

Days on market tells an interesting, slightly split story. The average days to sell actually crept up, nearly doubling from around 17 days to around 32. But the median days on market, which is a better read on what a typical listing experiences (average can get dragged around by a handful of stale, overpriced outliers), actually came down slightly, from around 14 days to 10. Translation: well-priced, well-presented homes are moving faster than ever, while a growing handful of overpriced or otherwise sticky listings are sitting long enough to pull the average upward. If you're selling, this is your signal. Price it right and stage it well, and you're likely still looking at a matter of days, not weeks. Overshoot on price, and you risk becoming one of the outliers dragging the average.

Sale-to-list price ratio stayed healthy and essentially unchanged, hovering around 100 to 104 percent both years. Homes here are still routinely selling at or slightly above asking.

## Twelve Months at a Glance

Zooming out from the month-to-month noise, here's what the full trailing year looked like for La Crescenta single family homes:

239 homes closed escrow, for a combined $343,928,395 in total sold volume. The average sale price came in at $1,439,031, with a median of $1,400,000, tight enough together that you can trust the median as a representative number rather than one skewed by a few mega-sales. Average price per square foot landed at $831.16. Homes sold, on average, in 27 days, and sold for 103.80% of their most recent list price, meaning the typical seller here isn't just meeting asking price, they're beating it.

The bulk of activity happened in the $1,000,001 to $2,000,000 range, which accounted for 191 of the 239 closed sales, over 80 percent of everything that sold. Three and four bedroom homes dominated that price band specifically. If you're a buyer trying to figure out where the real competition is, it's there, in that $1 to $2 million, 3 to 4 bedroom sweet spot. (And btw as a La Crescenta home owner this tracks. I've been there in the trenches of the bidding wars in this band and lived to tell the tale.)

Speed was also a defining feature of the year as a whole, not just July. 160 of the 239 sales, exactly two-thirds, closed within 30 days of hitting the market. Only 5 sat for longer than 120 days. This is not a market where you get to think it over for a month.

## The Condo Story: A Segment Waking Up

If single family homes had a story about tightening supply and steady prices, La Crescenta condos had a completely different story this year: a surge in both inventory and activity.

Active condo listings more than quadrupled over the past year, climbing from just 8 in August and September 2025 to 35 by June 2026. Closed sales followed the same arc, and then some. July 2026 alone saw 13 condo closings in La Crescenta, more than quadruple the handful that closed in July 2025, and easily the busiest single month for condos in the entire twelve month window.

Over the full trailing year, 44 condos closed for a combined $35,533,731, at an average price of $807,585 and a median of $806,125. Average days to sell ran a bit longer than single family, 35 days, and price per square foot averaged $589.48, meaningfully below the single family average, which tracks with what you'd expect from a smaller, more attached product type.

Here's the nuance worth understanding if you're watching this segment: per-unit price actually softened somewhat late in the window even as volume surged, which on the surface can look like condos are losing value. What's more likely happening, given the timing, is a mix shift. A wave of new, often smaller or more entry-level condo inventory hit the market and got absorbed quickly, pulling the per-unit averages down without necessarily meaning any individual seller took a bath. If you're a first-time buyer who's been priced out of single family inventory in the Foothills, this is worth watching closely. More condo inventory, moving fast, at a meaningfully lower price point than detached homes, is exactly the kind of opening first-time buyers should be paying attention to right now.

## How La Crescenta Stacks Up Against La Cañada Flintridge

I include La Cañada in these reports because buyers cross-shop the two constantly, and the contrast is a useful context, not just a neighboring data point. Plus, I love numbers so what's a few more?

Over the same twelve months, La Cañada Flintridge saw 176 single family homes close for a combined $496,050,473, at an average price of $2,818,469 and a median of $2,452,500. That's roughly 75 percent higher than La Crescenta's median. Average price per square foot in La Cañada came in at $1,006.47, versus La Crescenta's $831.16, about a 21 percent premium per square foot. Days to sell ran similar between the two markets, 28 days average in La Cañada versus 27 in La Crescenta, and La Cañada's sale-to-list ratio, 101.51%, was actually a touch lower than La Crescenta's 103.80%, meaning La Crescenta sellers are, on average, getting slightly more aggressive competition relative to asking price than their La Cañada counterparts.

The condo comparison is even more lopsided, mostly because La Cañada simply doesn't have much condo stock to begin with. Only 9 condos closed there over the full year, compared to La Crescenta's 44, though at a higher median price, $1,400,000 versus $806,125.

The takeaway, if you're weighing the two: La Cañada carries a real and consistent price premium, both in total dollars and per square foot, without a corresponding advantage in speed or seller leverage. La Crescenta remains the higher-volume, faster-moving, and notably more attainable option in the immediate Foothills, particularly if a condo or a $1 to $2 million single family home is your actual budget range rather than an aspirational one.

## The Rental Market: What Leasing Data Tells Us About Demand

I don't usually dig into lease data for these updates, but it's a useful gut check on underlying demand, and the story it tells here is worth including.

In La Crescenta, 264 leases closed over the trailing year, at an average of $3,743 a month and a median of $3,795, with tenants and landlords agreeing to terms right around 99.52% of asking rent, essentially no negotiating room in either direction. Average days to lease ran 34 days. Comparing July 2026 to July 2025 directly, closed leases roughly doubled month over month, while the actual lease price barely moved. More renters were actively signing leases, but rents themselves held close to flat.

La Cañada's rental market is a different animal entirely. 102 leases closed at a median of $6,925 a month, nearly double La Crescenta's median, which tracks with everything we just covered on the sale side. If you're priced out of buying in La Cañada, you're also going to pay a steep premium to rent there instead.

The broader signal here: rental demand in La Crescenta stayed strong and fast-moving all year without rents actually climbing much. Combined with the for-sale data, that suggests the pressure in this market is coming from more people wanting to be here, not from a supply squeeze that's forcing prices in every direction at once. Worth noting too that mortgage rates were essentially flat over this same period, hovering in the mid to high 6 percent range in both August 2025 and August 2026, so none of what we're seeing here is a financing story. It's a demand story.

## What This Means If You're Buying

To paraphrase the daddest dad joke my dad used to say: "My name is Caila Ball, not Crystal Ball." So, while I unfortunately can't see the future, I can read the data and make an educated prediction: Don't wait for La Crescenta to get cheaper. Months of supply cutting in half over a year, combined with pending sales up 60-plus percent, tells you buyer competition intensified, not eased, even while price growth stayed relatively modest. That combination, more competition without runaway price growth, is arguably the best window a buyer gets in a market like this. It won't necessarily stay this way.

If single family homes in the $1 to $2 million range feel out of reach, take a real look at condos. That segment saw a genuine inventory bump this year and per-unit prices actually softened somewhat, which is a rare combination in this market right now.

And get pre-approved before you're seriously touring, not after. With two-thirds of single family sales closing inside 30 days, you don't have room to shop for financing mid-negotiation.

## What This Means If You're Selling

Pricing accuracy matters more than it did a year ago, not less. The split between a shrinking median days on market and a growing average tells you buyers are still rewarding well-priced homes with fast, competitive offers, but they're also increasingly willing to walk past anything that feels overpriced and let it sit. A year ago, more listings could get away with a stretch price and still sell reasonably fast. That margin for error has narrowed.

Presentation matters just as much. In a market where the fast movers are pulling further ahead of the slow movers, the homes that show well and price accurately from day one are the ones landing in that 30-day window at 103-plus percent of list.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is La Crescenta a buyer's or seller's market right now?

Based on the trailing twelve months of MLS data, La Crescenta has shifted toward favoring sellers, with months of supply tightening from around four months in mid-2025 to around two months by mid-2026. Anything under three months of supply is generally considered a seller's market.

### How much have home prices actually gone up in La Crescenta over the past year?

Modestly. Comparing July 2026 to July 2025, median single family sale prices rose roughly in the high single digits percentage-wise, landing around $1.4 to $1.55 million, even as buyer competition and sales volume increased more sharply.

### Is La Crescenta cheaper than La Cañada Flintridge?

Yes, significantly. Over the trailing twelve months, La Crescenta's median single family sale price was roughly 75 percent lower than La Cañada Flintridge's, and price per square foot ran about 21 percent lower, without a meaningful difference in how fast homes sold.

### Are condos a good option in La Crescenta right now?

The condo segment saw a real jump in both inventory and closed sales over the past year, with per-unit prices softening somewhat as more listings came onto the market. For buyers priced out of single family homes, it's worth a closer look than it might have been a year ago.

### How does renting in La Crescenta compare to La Cañada Flintridge?

Substantially cheaper. The median lease price in La Crescenta over the trailing year was $3,795 a month, compared to $6,925 in La Cañada Flintridge, nearly double.

## The TL;DR

La Crescenta didn't have a dramatic year on paper. Prices moved up gently instead of spiking, which is honestly the healthiest version of a strong market. But underneath that calm price line, competition clearly intensified: supply tightened, pending sales surged, and the gap between well-priced listings and everything else widened. Add in a condo segment that's opening up and a rental market absorbing more tenants without rents running away, and the overall picture is a Foothills market that's in demand without being in a bubble.
