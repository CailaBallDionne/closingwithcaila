Whether it's the plan from day one, or a strategy that comes up at some point during the search, focusing on condos often enters the picture for Los Angeles buyers, including those in the Foothills. Condos are easier to find in the Foothills than they were a year ago. In La Crescenta, [active condo listings went from 8 to 35 between late 2025 and mid 2026](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/la-crescenta-market-update-july-2026), at a median price well below the single family median.

Whether a condo is the right fit depends on your plans, your budget, and the specific building. Buying one also works differently than buying a house, and most of those differences live in the paperwork rather than the floor plan.

**When you buy a condo in California, you are buying your unit plus a share of a homeowners association, which means you inherit its budget, its reserves, its insurance, and its future repair bills. California law requires the seller to give you the HOA's governing documents, budget, reserve summary, insurance information, and any assessment and litigation disclosures before the sale. Read them during your contingency period, look closely at reserve funding and any planned special assessments, and have your lender confirm early that the building itself is eligible for your loan type, because condo projects are reviewed separately from you as a borrower.**

Let's go through it piece by piece.

## You Are Buying Two Things, Not One

(Because we love a BOGO, right?) With a single family home, you own the house and the land. When the roof needs replacing, you call a roofer and you pay the roofer.

With a condo, you own your unit, and you own a share of everything else together with your neighbors: the roof, the exterior walls, the hallways, the elevator, the pool, the parking structure, the balconies. The homeowners association is the entity that maintains all of it, and it pays for that maintenance with your monthly dues, plus whatever it has saved, plus, when those come up short, special assessments.

So the real question in a condo purchase is never just "do I like this unit?" It is also "is this association run well enough that I want to be financially tied to it?" That second question is answered almost entirely in paperwork.

## The Tradeoffs, Honestly

A condo is not automatically a better or worse purchase than a house. It is a different one, and the tradeoffs are worth weighing before you start touring.

**What tends to work in a condo's favor:**

- **A lower price of entry** in many neighborhoods, which can mean buying sooner or with a smaller down payment.
- **Less exterior upkeep on your plate,** since the association handles the roof, landscaping, and common areas.
- **Amenities and locations** that would be out of reach as a standalone house, like a pool, a gym, or a walkable spot near shops and transit.

**What tends to work against it:**

- **Monthly dues that can rise,** plus the possibility of special assessments when big repairs come due.
- **Less control.** You live under the association's rules, and decisions about the building are made by a board and a vote of owners, not by you alone.
- **Financing depends on the building, not just on you,** which can narrow your loan options in some projects.
- **Resale can take longer.** A [Redfin analysis of April 2026 data](https://keyt.com/?p=1446321) found that Los Angeles condos generally took longer to go under contract than the city's homes overall, and it pointed to rising HOA fees and insurance costs as part of the reason.

None of these is a dealbreaker on its own. How they balance out depends on how long you plan to stay, how you feel about shared decision-making, and, more than anything, how well the specific building is run.

## What the Seller Has to Give You

California does not leave this to chance. Under [Civil Code Section 4525](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=4525), the seller of a condo has to provide the buyer with a defined set of HOA documents before the sale. In practice this arrives as a resale package from the association or its management company, and it typically includes:

- **The governing documents.** The CC&Rs, bylaws, articles of incorporation, and operating rules. These are the rules you are agreeing to live under, covering everything from pets and parking to renovations and rentals.
- **The annual budget report and the most recent financial statement review.** Where the money comes from, where it goes, and what is left over.
- **The reserve study summary and the Assessment and Reserve Funding Disclosure Summary.** How much the association has saved for major repairs, and how much it should have.
- **Assessment information.** Current dues, plus any special assessments that have been approved or are planned.
- **Insurance summaries.** What the association's master policy covers, and what it doesn't.
- **Any notices of pending litigation or construction defect claims.**
- **A statement of any rental restrictions,** if the governing documents have them.
- **Approved board meeting minutes from the prior 12 months,** if you ask for them. Which you really, really should.

Let's focus on minutes for a…minute, because that's the item in this list that you have to request. Minutes are where the interesting stuff lives: the roof that the board has been "monitoring" for three years, the plumbing issue that keeps coming back, the insurance renewal that just came in way over budget. If you are buying a condo, ask for the minutes. Every time.

All of this should land during your [HOA document review contingency](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/what-are-real-estate-contingencies), which is the window in your contract where you can review what you received and walk away if something is a dealbreaker. That window has a deadline, and these packages can run to hundreds of pages, so it pays to start reading the day they arrive rather than the night before. The HOA package also arrives alongside the seller's own disclosures, like the TDS and the Natural Hazard Disclosure, which I break down in [what each California seller disclosure covers](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/california-seller-disclosures-tds-spq-avid-nhd).

## How to Read the Money Part Without a Finance Degree

You do not need to audit the association. You need to answer three questions.

### Is the association collecting enough to run the building?

Look at the annual budget. Are dues covering normal operating costs, or is the association running a deficit and dipping into savings to make ends meet? A budget that only balances because nobody has repainted anything in a decade is not actually balanced.

### Has it saved enough for the big stuff?

This is what the reserve study tells you. Under [Civil Code Section 5550](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=5550), most associations must have a visual inspection of their major components done at least once every three years as part of a reserve study, and the board has to review that study every year. The study lists the big-ticket items the association is responsible for, like roofs, paving, elevators, plumbing, and painting, estimates how many years each one has left, and estimates what replacing them will cost.

The number most people zero in on is the **percent funded**: how much the association actually has in reserves compared to what the study says it should have at this point. There is no legal minimum, and different professionals use different benchmarks, so treat any single cutoff as a rule of thumb rather than a rule. The direction matters as much as the number. A building at 45 percent funded with a realistic plan to catch up is a different situation from a building at 45 percent that has been sliding for five years.

### What is coming due soon?

Read the reserve study's component list with an eye on the next five years. If the roof has two years of useful life left and the reserves can't cover it, someone is going to pay for that roof. Increasingly, that someone is the owners, through a special assessment.

## Special Assessments: The Bill You Didn't Budget For

A special assessment is a one-time (or short-term) charge the association levies on every owner, on top of regular dues, usually to pay for a repair the reserves can't cover.

California puts some guardrails on this. Under [Civil Code Section 5605](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=5605), an HOA board can't raise regular dues more than 20 percent over the prior year, or impose special assessments that add up to more than 5 percent of the association's budgeted gross expenses for the year, without a vote of the owners. There are [exceptions for genuine emergencies](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=5610), such as an extraordinary expense needed to repair conditions that threaten personal safety, so the 5 percent cap is a guardrail, not a guarantee.

Here is what that means in plain terms: a vote of the owners can approve a much larger assessment, and in an emergency the board may not need a vote at all. If the building needs a $2 million repair and has $400,000 saved, the gap gets filled one way or another.

### Why balconies are part of this conversation now

One California-specific driver of special assessments in recent years is the balcony inspection law. Under [Civil Code Section 5551](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=5551), condo associations have to have a licensed structural or civil engineer or architect inspect a sample of their exterior elevated elements, meaning balconies, decks, walkways, and stairs supported by wood, at least once every nine years. The first round for existing buildings was due January 1, 2025, according to the [California Association of REALTORS® quick guide on balcony inspections](https://www.car.org/-/media/CAR/Documents/Transaction-Center/PDF/QUICK-GUIDES/Quick-Guide--Balcony-Inspection-Laws---Deadline-January-1-2025-REVISED-103124.pdf).

That means most associations with wood-supported balconies or walkways should now have an inspection report on file. If you are looking at a building like that, it is worth asking whether the inspection was done, what it found, and whether repairs have been budgeted. An inspection that recommended work, combined with thin reserves, is exactly how a special assessment gets born.

### Who pays an assessment that's already been approved?

This is negotiable, and it is worth negotiating. If an assessment has been approved but is being paid in installments, the default is usually that whoever owns the unit when each installment comes due pays it. A buyer can ask the seller to pay off the remaining balance at closing, or to credit the buyer for it. Find out about the assessment early, and it becomes part of the price conversation instead of a surprise after you get the keys.

## Insurance: Know Where the Building's Policy Stops

Every condo association carries a master insurance policy on the building and common areas. You will typically carry your own condo policy, often called an HO-6, on top of that. The tricky part is the seam between them.

Some master policies cover the building down to the bare studs and leave everything inside your unit to you. Others cover more of the interior. Which one your building has changes how much coverage you need on your own policy, and it is spelled out in the insurance summary in your resale package. This is a question for your insurance agent or broker, ideally before you remove your contingencies, because they can read the master policy summary and tell you what gaps you need to fill.

Two things make this especially important in California right now.

**First, association insurance has gotten harder to place and more expensive.** As traditional insurers have pulled back, more associations have turned to the California FAIR Plan. The Department of Insurance [raised the FAIR Plan's commercial property coverage limit to $20 million per building](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/0400-news/0100-press-releases/2025/release028-2025.cfm) in 2025, explicitly to help HOAs that had been priced out or left without coverage. When an association's insurance costs jump, that increase flows into everyone's dues. Look at the budget for how much insurance has gone up recently, and check the minutes for anything about upcoming renewals.

**Second, earthquakes.** Standard condo policies generally don't cover earthquake damage, and many associations don't carry a master earthquake policy at all. The [California Earthquake Authority's condo policy](https://earthquakeauthority.com/California-Earthquake-Insurance-Policies/Condominium) includes an optional coverage called loss assessment, which helps pay your share if the association assesses owners for earthquake repairs or to cover its own earthquake deductible. Whether that makes sense for you is a conversation for an insurance professional, but you should at least know it exists before you need it.

## The Building Has to Qualify for Your Loan, Too

This is the part that catches buyers off guard most often. When you finance a condo, your lender doesn't just evaluate you. They also evaluate the project, meaning the whole building or complex. A buyer with excellent credit and a solid down payment can still hit a wall because the building doesn't meet the guidelines for their loan type.

I want to be careful here, because loan eligibility is your lender's territory, not mine, and the specifics change. But the general shape is worth understanding so you know which questions to ask early.

**Conventional loans.** Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac set standards for condo projects, and a building that doesn't meet them is often called "non-warrantable." Fannie Mae's guide lists [characteristics that make a project ineligible](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b4-2.1-03/ineligible-projects), including more than 35 percent of the space being used commercially, a need for critical repairs or significant deferred maintenance, and certain kinds of pending litigation. In LA, that commercial space limit is worth keeping in mind with mixed-use buildings that have ground-floor retail. Non-warrantable doesn't automatically mean you can't buy there, but it can mean different loan products, different terms, and a smaller pool of lenders.

**FHA loans.** FHA approves condo projects, and you can check a building's status on HUD's condo search tool. If a building isn't on the list, HUD also allows a [single-unit approval process](https://archives.hud.gov/news/2019/pr19-121.cfm) for certain units in non-approved projects, as long as the project meets FHA's requirements.

**VA loans.** VA keeps its own list of approved condo projects, separate from FHA's. If the building isn't on it, getting it approved can add real time to a transaction, so that is a question to settle before writing an offer, not after.

The practical takeaway: before you get attached to a specific building, send the address to your lender and ask whether it works for your loan type. Your lender will likely send the association a questionnaire as part of underwriting, and the answers can change the picture, so the earlier that process starts, the better. If you haven't connected with a lender yet, my post on [pre-approval versus pre-qualification](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/pre-approval-vs-pre-qualification) is a good place to start.

## Dues Count Against What You Can Afford

HOA dues are a monthly housing cost, and lenders generally count them when calculating how much house you qualify for. A condo with a lower price and high dues can end up costing about the same per month as a pricier condo with low dues. Your lender can run the actual numbers for you, and it is worth asking them to model a couple of different dues amounts before you start comparing buildings.

Dues are also worth looking at as information, not just as a cost. Very low dues can be a sign of a well-run, efficient building. They can also be a sign that the association has been keeping dues artificially low by underfunding reserves, which tends to catch up with everyone eventually. The reserve study is how you tell the difference.

## If You Might Rent It Out Someday

Lots of buyers buy a condo planning to live in it for a few years and then keep it as a rental. California law helps here. Under [Civil Code Section 4741](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=4741), an HOA generally can't prohibit or unreasonably restrict owners from renting their units, and can't cap rentals at less than 25 percent of the units. Associations can, however, ban short-term rentals of 30 days or less.

So a long-term rental plan is usually workable, though [local rules on rent increases and evictions](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/rent-control-duplex-buyers-los-angeles-foothills) may apply to a unit you rent out, depending on where it sits. An Airbnb plan may not be. Read the CC&Rs and the rental rules in your resale package for the specifics, and keep in mind that city rules on short-term rentals apply on top of whatever the association allows. Also worth knowing: if you're thinking about financing as an owner-occupant and renting later, talk to your lender about what that means for your loan, since occupancy requirements are part of what you're agreeing to.

## What I Would Want to Know Before Writing an Offer

You won't have the full resale package until you're in contract, but you can get a surprising amount ahead of time with a mix of MLS research and asking the listing agent. Before writing an offer on a condo, I'd want to know:

- **The current monthly dues,** and whether any increase has been announced for next year.
- **Whether any special assessments are approved, pending, or being discussed,** and how much is left to pay.
- **The building's percent funded,** or at least a rough sense of the reserve picture.
- **Whether the building works for the buyer's loan type,** confirmed by the buyer's lender.
- **Whether there's any pending litigation** involving the association.
- **For buildings with wood-supported balconies or walkways, whether the balcony inspection has been done** and what it found.
- **The rental rules,** if renting it out later is part of the plan.

None of these answers has to be perfect. Almost every association has something going on. What you want is to understand what you're buying into, price it accordingly, and avoid finding out after closing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What HOA documents does a seller have to provide when selling a condo in California?

California Civil Code Section 4525 requires the seller to provide the HOA's governing documents (CC&Rs, bylaws, articles, and operating rules), the most recent budget report and financial statement review, the reserve and assessment disclosure summary, insurance information, notices of current or approved assessments, any litigation notices, and a statement of any rental restrictions. The prior 12 months of approved board meeting minutes must also be provided if the buyer requests them.

### What is a reserve study, and what does "percent funded" mean?

A reserve study is a report that lists the major components an HOA is responsible for, such as roofs, paving, and plumbing, and estimates their remaining life and replacement cost. In California, most associations must have a visual inspection of those components at least every three years and review the study annually. Percent funded compares the reserves the association actually has to what the study says it should have saved by now. There is no legal minimum, so look at the trend and the funding plan along with the number.

### How much can a California HOA raise dues or impose a special assessment without a vote?

Under California Civil Code Section 5605, a board generally can't increase regular assessments by more than 20 percent over the prior year, or impose special assessments totaling more than 5 percent of the association's budgeted gross expenses for the year, without approval from the owners. Larger assessments can be approved by an owner vote, and California law also allows certain emergency assessments without a vote.

### What is a non-warrantable condo?

A non-warrantable condo is generally one in a building that doesn't meet Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac's project standards, which affects conventional financing. Common reasons include too much commercial space, critical repairs or significant deferred maintenance, certain pending litigation, or ownership concentrated in a single entity. Buying one is often still possible, but it may require a different loan product, so ask your lender to review the specific building early.

### Can I use an FHA or VA loan to buy a condo?

Often, yes, but the building generally has to be approved for that loan type. FHA keeps a list of approved condo projects and also allows single-unit approval for some units in non-approved projects. VA keeps its own separate list. Your lender can check a building's status and tell you whether approval is possible and how long it might take.

### Can my condo HOA stop me from renting out my unit?

In California, generally not for long-term rentals. Civil Code Section 4741 prevents HOAs from prohibiting or unreasonably restricting rentals and from capping rentals at less than 25 percent of the units. HOAs can, however, prohibit short-term rentals of 30 days or less, and local short-term rental rules apply too.

## The Bottom Line

A condo can be a smart, realistic way to buy in Los Angeles, especially in the Foothills, where it is often the difference between owning in the neighborhood you love and renting in it for another five years. The trick is remembering that you are joining an association, not just buying a unit. Read the documents, ask for the minutes, look hard at the reserves and any planned assessments, and get your lender's blessing on the building before you fall in love with the view.
