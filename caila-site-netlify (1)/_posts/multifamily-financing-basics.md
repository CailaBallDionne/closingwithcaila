**Financing a multifamily property depends almost entirely on two things: how many units it has, and whether you plan to live in one of them. Properties with two to four units can qualify for residential-style loans, including FHA with as little as 3.5% down if you're owner-occupying. Once a building hits five units, it's treated as commercial real estate and gets underwritten on the property's income, not yours.**

"Multifamily financing" gets talked about like it's one thing. It isn't. It's three or four different games wearing the same name tag, and which one applies to you comes down to two questions: how many units, and are you living in one of them.

I get asked about this a lot, from people wanting to house hack their first duplex to folks building a small portfolio. So let's break down what your actual options look like, because the loan you'd use for a fourplex you're moving into is not the loan you'd use for a ten-unit building you're not.

## The Line That Changes Everything: 4 Units vs. 5 Units

This is the one rule that matters more than any other here. Properties with one to four units are still considered residential for lending purposes, even if every unit but yours is rented out. That means FHA, VA, or conventional financing are all on the table, the same loan products you'd use to buy a single-family house.

The moment a property hits five units, it crosses into commercial real estate. Underwriting shifts from your personal income and debt-to-income ratio to the property's own numbers: debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) and loan-to-value (LTV). Different paperwork, different timelines, different lenders entirely.

We'll cover both sides, starting with the 2-4 unit world, where most first-time multifamily buyers live.

## Option 1: FHA, If You're Living There Too

If you're planning to live in one unit and rent the others, FHA is usually the cheapest way in the door. Because FHA treats a duplex, triplex, or fourplex the same as a single-family home when you're owner-occupying, you can qualify with as little as 3.5% down with a credit score of 580 or higher.

A few things to know before you get attached to this plan:

- **Occupancy timeline:** You have to move in within 60 days of closing and live there for at least a year. This isn't a buy-it-rent-all-four-and-vacation-in-Cabo strategy.
- **Self-sufficiency test:** Triplexes and fourplexes have to pass a self-sufficiency test, where 75% of the building's market rent has to cover the full mortgage payment. Duplexes are exempt.
- **Loan limits:** FHA limits are set by county and adjust yearly. The 2026 national baseline runs roughly $671,200 for a duplex up to just over $1 million for a fourplex, but LA County is a high-cost area, so its 2026 ceilings are considerably higher: $1,599,375 for a duplex, $1,933,200 for a triplex, and $2,402,625 for a fourplex. Your lender pulls the exact number for your specific area.
- **Mortgage insurance:** Mortgage insurance sticks around for the life of the loan if you put down less than 10%. Most people refinance it away once they hit 20% equity.

This is the classic "house hacking" move: live in one unit, let the tenants cover a meaningful chunk of your mortgage, and build equity in a property that's doing double duty as your home and your first rental portfolio.

## Option 2: Conventional Financing for 2-4 Units

Conventional loans split hard depending on whether you're living there.

- **Owner-occupied:** Fannie Mae rolled out a 5% down payment option for owner-occupied 2-4 unit properties, a real drop from the 15-25% it used to require. There's no self-sufficiency test like FHA requires, and you can use projected rental income from the other units to help you qualify. You'll need a credit score of at least 620, six months of mortgage payments in reserve after your down payment and closing costs, and PMI on the loan until you build enough equity to drop it. So this isn't a program for buyers cutting it close at the closing table.
- **Non-owner-occupied (pure investment):** If you're not living there, expect to put down 25%, the standard for a 2-4 unit investment property under Fannie Mae guidelines. Lenders will also want a credit score of 680 or higher (720+ gets better terms), a debt-to-income ratio generally capped around 45%, and 3-12 months of cash reserves depending on the lender and your file.

## Option 3: DSCR Loans, for When Your Income Isn't the Point

This is the one that trips people up, in a good way. A DSCR loan (debt service coverage ratio) qualifies you based on what the property earns, not what you earn. No W-2s, no tax returns, no employment verification. The lender looks at the property's projected rental income relative to its mortgage payment and decides from there.

This matters a lot for two kinds of buyers: self-employed investors whose tax returns don't reflect their actual cash flow (write-offs are great for April, less great for mortgage applications), and anyone whose personal debt-to-income ratio is already maxed out from other properties.

The tradeoff here is rate. DSCR loans tend to run .75 to 1.5 percentage points higher than conventional loans. And DSCR loans are investor-only. There's no owner-occupied version, so if you're planning to live in one of the units, this isn't your program.

## Once You Hit 5 Units, Welcome to Commercial

Everything changes here. You're now looking at Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac agency loans, HUD 223(f) financing, or bridge loans if you need to move fast or the property needs work before it qualifies for permanent financing. Agency loans typically take 45 to 90 days to close, noticeably longer than the residential timelines you're used to.

Underwriting is all about the building's own performance: DSCR, LTV, in-place occupancy, and market rents. Your personal income barely enters the conversation. For context on how active this market is, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac's combined 2026 multifamily loan purchase caps sit at $176 billion, a 20.5% jump from 2025, so the appetite to lend on this asset class is real.

This is also where things get complicated, and where working with a lender who specializes in commercial multifamily, not your everyday residential loan officer, matters.

## So Which One Is Actually Right for You?

- **Want to live in it and minimize cash out of pocket?** FHA, 3.5% down, understanding you're committing to a year of owner-occupancy.
- **Want to live in it but skip the self-sufficiency test and use rental income to qualify?** Fannie Mae's 5% down owner-occupied program.
- **Buying a pure rental and want the best long-term terms?** Conventional investment financing at 25% down.
- **Self-employed, DTI-maxed, or just tired of the income documentation dance?** A DSCR loan, with a bigger down payment as the tradeoff.
- **Eyeing a 5+ unit building?** You're in commercial territory now, and it's worth talking to both a commercial lender and an agent who's dealt with income properties before you write an offer.

Worth noting: lower down payment financing, FHA especially, can make an offer less competitive in a hot seller's market. FHA's stricter appraisal and property condition requirements read as more risk to a seller, so in a multiple-offer situation, a buyer with 20% down and conventional financing often has the edge.

None of these is objectively the best loan. They're the best for a specific situation, and the right call depends on your timeline, your cash position, and whether you're planning to live in the building or not.

## FAQ

**What's the minimum down payment for a multifamily property?**
It depends on the loan type and occupancy. FHA owner-occupied starts at 3.5%, Fannie Mae's newer owner-occupied program starts at 5%, non-owner-occupied conventional financing typically requires 25%, and DSCR loans run 20-30%.

**What's the difference between a DSCR loan and a conventional loan?**
Conventional loans qualify you based on your personal income, credit, and debt-to-income ratio. DSCR loans qualify based on the property's rental income relative to its mortgage payment, with no personal income documentation required.

**Can I buy a fourplex with an FHA loan?**
Yes, as long as you'll live in one of the units as your primary residence for at least a year. Fourplexes also have to pass a self-sufficiency test, where 75% of market rent covers the full mortgage payment.

**At what point does a property become "commercial" for financing purposes?**
Five units is the line. One to four units get financed with residential loan products (FHA, conventional, DSCR); five or more units move into commercial underwriting based on DSCR and LTV.

**Do DSCR loans require proof of income?**
No. DSCR loans qualify based on the property's cash flow, not your personal income, employment history, or tax returns.
