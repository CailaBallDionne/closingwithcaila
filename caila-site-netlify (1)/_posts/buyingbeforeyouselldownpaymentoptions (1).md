When my family started looking to move from our first home in Cypress Park to La Crescenta, we ran into the same wall almost every move-up buyer hits eventually: nearly everything we had was sitting inside the house we hadn't sold yet. On paper, we looked like we had plenty of equity to work with. In practice, that equity was locked behind a closing that hadn't happened yet, and we needed a down payment now, not in three months.

If that sounds familiar, you're not doing anything wrong. This is just what happens when your net worth and your cash are two very different things.

**When most of your money is tied up in the equity of your current home, your realistic options for funding a down payment on your next one are a HELOC, a bridge loan, or a Buy Before You Sell program like HomeLight's, and in a competitive market, an offer contingent on selling your current house first is rarely going to be the one that wins.**

Let's get into what that actually means, option by option.

## The Problem: Your Net Worth Isn't Your Down Payment

This catches a lot of move-up buyers off guard, especially the first time they go through it. You've built real equity, maybe hundreds of thousands of dollars of it, but that equity isn't sitting in a checking account. It's locked into a specific asset, and turning it into usable cash requires either selling that asset or borrowing against it.

That's the whole puzzle. You need money to buy the next house, but the money is trapped inside the house you're leaving. Three things can untrap it: sell first and rent or stay with family in between, make an offer contingent on your current home selling, or borrow against the equity you already have so you can buy first and sell on your own timeline.

Each of those has a real tradeoff. Let's talk about why the second one, the contingent offer, is the one I'd steer most Foothills buyers (and LA buyers in general) away from leaning on.

## Why a Sale-Contingent Offer Isn't a Realistic Plan Here

A sale contingency means your purchase depends on successfully selling your current home first. I've written before about [how this contingency works mechanically](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/what-are-real-estate-contingencies), including the kick-out clause that lets a seller keep marketing their home and accept a better offer if one comes along while you're still trying to sell yours.

Here's the honest version for anyone house hunting in La Crescenta, La Cañada Flintridge, or the rest of the Foothills right now: sellers can and do simply pass on offers with a sale contingency attached when they have other options, and in [this market, they usually do](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/la-crescenta-market-update-july-2026). Months of supply here tightened to around two months over the past year, well into seller-favorable territory, and two-thirds of single family home sales closed within 30 days of hitting the market. When a listing is fielding multiple offers in its first weekend, a seller has zero incentive to accept a deal that depends on your house selling too, when there's a clean, financing-ready offer sitting right next to it.

Listen: a sale-contingent offer is the option that sounds the most obvious and the least risky on paper: you sell, then you buy, nothing has to be borrowed. In a slower market, or with a motivated seller who isn't fielding other offers, it can work. In the Foothills right now, leaning on it as your plan is more likely to cost you the house than protect you financially. If this is truly your only path forward, it changes how we structure your search and your offers, and it's a conversation worth having with your agent upfront rather than after you've lost two houses to cleaner offers.

That leaves borrowing against the equity you already have, so you can make a strong, non-contingent offer now and sell on a timeline that isn't dictated by someone else's closing date. There are three real ways to do that.

## Option 1: A HELOC on Your Current Home

A home equity line of credit (HELOC) lets you borrow against the equity in your current home while you still own it, draw against it like a credit line, and use that cash toward the down payment on your next purchase. This is what my family used moving from NELA to La Crescenta.

Here's the mechanics:

- **How much you can borrow:** Most lenders cap a HELOC at somewhere between 80% and 85% of your home's value, combined with what you still owe on your existing mortgage ([The Mortgage Reports](https://themortgagereports.com/96211/maximum-heloc-amount)). So the math starts with your home's current value, subtracts your existing mortgage balance, and what's left (up to that 80-85% ceiling) is roughly your available credit line.
- **What it costs:** HELOC rates are variable and tied to market conditions. As of mid-August 2026, the national average HELOC rate was running around 7.31%, with actual rates ranging from roughly 4% to nearly 12% depending on your credit, your lender, and your combined loan-to-value ([Bankrate](https://www.bankrate.com/home-equity/heloc-rates/)). You'll also see typical closing costs, though many lenders waive or reduce them to win your business.
- **How it behaves:** A HELOC has a draw period (commonly 10 years) where you can borrow, repay, and borrow again, followed by a repayment period. For a move-up purchase, you typically draw what you need for your down payment, then pay the HELOC off in full once your old home sells, often within weeks or a few months.

The appeal of a HELOC is flexibility. You're not taking out a lump-sum loan you don't need in full; you're opening a credit line and drawing against it. If your old home sells faster than expected, you pay it off faster and the interest cost shrinks accordingly. The tradeoff is that you need your current mortgage lender or a new one to actually approve the HELOC before you're under contract on the new place, which means this isn't a same-week solution. Start the HELOC application process before you're seriously touring homes, not after you've found the one.

## Option 2: A Bridge Loan

A bridge loan is a short-term loan secured by your current home, designed specifically to cover the down payment gap between buying your next house and selling your current one. Where a HELOC is a flexible credit line, a bridge loan is typically a lump sum built for one job: get you to the closing table on your new home.

The mechanics look different from a HELOC in a few important ways:

- **Cost:** Bridge loan rates typically run higher than a HELOC, commonly in the 6% to 12% range, along with closing costs and fees usually running 1% to 3% of the loan amount ([LendingTree](https://www.lendingtree.com/home/mortgage/bridge-financing-basics/)).
- **Term:** Bridge loans are built to be short. Most require repayment within 6 to 12 months, which lines up with the assumption that your old home will sell relatively quickly.
- **Qualification:** Lenders generally want to see meaningful equity in your current home, often around 20%, and they'll look hard at your combined debt-to-income ratio since you may technically be carrying two mortgage-sized obligations for a stretch ([LendingTree](https://www.lendingtree.com/home/mortgage/bridge-financing-basics/)).

Bridge loans tend to make the most sense when speed and certainty matter more than cost, you need a defined lump sum for a down payment, you're confident your current home will sell within that 6 to 12 month window, and you'd rather have a fixed structure than a credit line you have to manage yourself. They also tend to be the more expensive option of the two, so this is very much a "what does your specific situation need" conversation rather than a default pick.

## Option 3: A "Buy Before You Sell" Program (Like HomeLight's)

Over the last few years, a newer category of program has shown up specifically to solve this problem: services like HomeLight's Buy Before You Sell, along with similar offerings from companies like Knock, Orchard, and Homeward. These aren't a HELOC or a bridge loan you shop for at a bank; they're a packaged program run through a certified network of agents and lenders.

Here's how HomeLight's version works, specifically:

- **How it unlocks equity:** HomeLight evaluates your current home and existing mortgage, then tells you how much equity you can unlock toward your next down payment, up to $2 million, using their own underwriting rather than a standard HELOC or bridge loan application ([HomeLight](https://help.homelight.com/hc/en-us/articles/7279245128215-how-homelight-buy-before-you-sell-works)).
- **How you use it:** With that equity unlocked, you make a non-contingent offer on your new home. Once you close, you move in right away, and your old home is listed vacant, which tends to help it show and sell better than a lived-in home would.
- **The timeline:** Your agent typically has a short window to list your old home, and then a matter of months to get it sold. HomeLight's own backstop is the notable part here: if your old home hasn't sold by the deadline, they'll purchase it from you directly at the loan payoff value, so you're not left holding two homes indefinitely ([HomeLight](https://help.homelight.com/hc/en-us/articles/7279245128215-how-homelight-buy-before-you-sell-works)).
- **What it costs:** Rather than paying interest like you would on a HELOC or bridge loan, you pay a flat program fee based on your old home's eventual sale price, commonly cited around 2.4%, often reduced if you also use HomeLight's own lending and closing services ([RealEstateBees](https://realestatebees.com/company/homelight/buy-before-you-sell/)). Always confirm the current fee directly, since these programs adjust pricing by region and change it over time.

The tradeoff worth knowing upfront: you can't just walk into any lender and ask for this. Buy Before You Sell programs run through a certified network of participating agents and loan officers, so access depends on working with someone plugged into that program rather than a straightforward bank application. It's also worth comparing the flat program fee against what you'd actually pay in interest on a HELOC or bridge loan for your specific numbers and timeline, since which one comes out cheaper depends entirely on your equity position and how fast your home is likely to sell.

## Comparing All Three: The Short Version

- A HELOC is generally the cheapest and most flexible option, but takes longer to set up and requires you to actively manage a credit line yourself.
- A bridge loan is faster to structure around a specific purchase and simpler in concept, a lump sum, one payoff event, but it costs more in both rate and fees than a HELOC.
- A Buy Before You Sell program trades interest for a flat fee, adds a built-in safety net if your home doesn't sell in time, and can simplify the process considerably, but it requires working through that program's specific agent and lender network, and the fee needs to be weighed against what a HELOC or bridge loan would actually cost you.
- All three require real, meaningful equity in your current home. None of them are a fit if you're only a few years into your mortgage and haven't built up much of a cushion yet.
- All three are meant to be temporary. This isn't long-term debt or an ongoing cost, it's a bridge (fittingly) between two transactions.

## What Actually Made the HELOC Work for Us

When we were moving from Cypress Park to La Crescenta, we already had a mortgage lender relationship and enough equity built up that a HELOC was the more straightforward, less expensive path. We opened the line, drew what we needed for the down payment, made a clean, non-contingent offer on the La Crescenta house, and paid the HELOC down once Cypress Park closed. No kick-out clause, no waiting on someone else's buyer to perform, no losing a house we loved to a cleaner offer.

Now, if I'm being real real, there were some downsides (mostly related to my sanity). We closed on our new house in late August, so in order to get our house refreshed, staged, and on the market in a prime buying season, we had to get out of our departing house FAST. Like...pack up the house in 72 hours because we had a vacation booked, forego sleep sort of fast. I also don't love carrying debt (who does?), so there was some pretty steep "will the house really sell quickly and for as much as we're projecting?" anxiety for the brief period when we still owned both houses.

Ultimately, it worked out just fine and I lived to tell the tale. But I've been where you're sitting, I know what it's like to be a human in these financial transactions, and I don't take the responsibility of talking you through your options lightly.

## A Few Things to Sort Out Before You Start Touring

Before you're seriously house hunting with one of these three tools as your plan, a few things are worth nailing down with your lender and your agent together:

- Get a realistic read on your current home's value and how much usable equity that actually translates to, before you assume a number
- Start the HELOC, bridge loan, or Buy Before You Sell approval process before you're under contract, not after
- Understand exactly how you'll be qualified for the new purchase while your old mortgage is technically still on the books (this is where your debt-to-income ratio matters most)
- Have a real conversation about your current home's timeline to sell, since all three of these tools work best when that sale happens reasonably close to plan
- If a Buy Before You Sell program is on the table, confirm you're working with an agent and lender who are actually part of that program's certified network, and run the numbers on the flat fee versus what a HELOC or bridge loan would cost you

If you're also still getting oriented on how the buying process works more broadly, [these are the questions worth asking any agent](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/questions-first-time-homebuyers-should-ask-real-estate-agent) before you commit to working together, and [this covers what your agent agreement actually spells out](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/buyer-broker-representation-agreement-california).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I make an offer on a house before my current home sells?

Yes, but you generally need a way to fund your down payment without waiting for that sale to close first. A HELOC, a bridge loan, or a Buy Before You Sell program can all let you access your current home's equity so you can make a clean, non-contingent offer and sell on your own timeline afterward.

### Is a sale-contingent offer a bad idea?

It depends heavily on your market. In a competitive market like the Foothills, where homes routinely receive multiple offers and sell within weeks, sellers often pass on sale-contingent offers in favor of cleaner ones, even at a similar price. In a slower market with less competition, it can be a workable strategy.

### What is the difference between a HELOC and a bridge loan?

A HELOC is a revolving credit line secured by your current home, typically with lower rates and more flexibility. A bridge loan is a lump-sum, short-term loan built specifically to cover the gap between buying and selling, usually with a higher rate and more fees, but a simpler, faster structure.

### How much equity do I need for a HELOC or bridge loan?

Most lenders cap a HELOC at around 80% to 85% of your home's value combined with your existing mortgage balance. Bridge loans generally require around 20% equity, along with a debt-to-income ratio that can support carrying both properties temporarily.

### How does a Buy Before You Sell program like HomeLight's work?

Programs like HomeLight's Buy Before You Sell evaluate your current home's equity and unlock a portion of it so you can make a non-contingent offer and move into your new home before your old one sells. Instead of paying loan interest, you pay a flat program fee based on your old home's eventual sale price, and if it hasn't sold within the program's window, the company typically buys it from you directly as a backstop. Access runs through a certified network of participating agents and lenders rather than a standard bank application.

### When should I start the HELOC, bridge loan, or Buy Before You Sell process?

Before you're seriously touring homes. All three require approval that takes time, and you want that financing in place so you can move quickly and confidently the moment you find the right house.

## The Bottom Line

If your down payment feels stuck inside a house you haven't sold yet, you're not out of options, you just need the right tool for your specific situation. A HELOC, a bridge loan, or a Buy Before You Sell program can all turn equity you already have into a down payment you can actually use, so you're not stuck betting your next home on someone else's buyer showing up on time.
