An open house is the one time a home is on its best behavior. The beds are made, a candle is burning, and the throw pillows have been arranged at an angle by someone with strong opinions.

Enjoy it. And then look past the staging, because the aesthetic stack of coffee table hardcovers is not going to tell you whether the roof is nearing the end of its life.

The good news is you do not need to be an inspector. A professional inspection comes later, and it is a different job. At an open house, your job is smaller: figure out whether this home deserves a second look, and collect the questions worth asking before you spend money on anything else.

**At an open house, look past the staging for signs of water damage, structural movement, aging big-ticket systems, and drainage and fire-hardening details, then ask the listing agent about disclosures, permits, and the seller's timeline. You are not inspecting the home. You are deciding whether it earns a private showing and a professional inspection.**

Here is the short version:

- **Outside:** roofline and roof age, where water goes, and fire-hardening details
- **Ceilings and walls:** stains, rings, and one oddly fresh patch of paint
- **Doors, windows, and floors:** anything that sticks, gaps, or slopes
- **Systems:** the age of the water heater, AC, and furnace
- **Light, sound, and layout:** how it feels at this time of day
- **Questions:** disclosures, permits, recent work, and the seller's timeline

The rest of this post breaks each one down.

## First, a Quick Note on How Open Houses Work

You can walk into an open house without an agent.

So be friendly, and keep your budget, your timeline, and how much you love the kitchen to yourself. That is a good habit, not a rule. If you are already working with an agent, mention it at the door. And if you are not working with one yet, my post on the [buyer-broker representation agreement](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/buyer-broker-representation-agreement-california) explains the written agreement that comes into play once you are.

## Before You Go

A few minutes of prep makes the visit more useful:

**Read the listing.** Check how long it has been on the market and whether the price has changed, and compare it to [where the market stands right now](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/la-crescenta-market-update-september-2026).

**Drive the block at a different time of day.** [A drive-by is a quick way to see how the house sits on its lot](https://www.thisoldhouse.com/inspections/what-to-look-for-before-the-home-inspector-arrives): whether the land slopes toward it, and whether the roofline looks straight. Rush hour and a quiet Sunday can feel like two different streets.

**Pack light.** Your phone for notes and a small flashlight are plenty. Ask before taking photos, since some sellers prefer none. I'm vaguely obsessed with etiquette, so that's more of a manners tip as opposed to a rule.

## From the Curb: What to Check Outside

**The roof.** Look for missing or damaged shingles and any dips or bumps in the roofline. For age, [InterNACHI's estimated life expectancy chart](https://www.nachi.org/life-expectancy.htm) lists about 20 years for 3-tab asphalt shingles and about 30 for architectural ones, and it notes that hot climates (hello, SoCal) cut asphalt shingle life significantly. Those numbers are guidelines, not guarantees, but they are a good reason to ask when the roof was last replaced.

**Where the water goes.** Look at gutters and downspouts, soil piled against siding, and any standing water or erosion. On a hillside lot, look at retaining walls and think about where water would travel in a heavy rain. Cracks, leaning, or pooling are worth pointing out to your inspector later.

**Fire hardening.** If the home is in or near a fire hazard zone, notice what you can see from outside. [California's Safer from Wildfires regulation](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/200-wrr/Safer-from-Wildfires.cfm) lists steps like a Class A fire-rated roof, ember-resistant vents, enclosed eaves, upgraded windows, and a 5-foot ember-resistant zone as the mitigation that qualifies for insurance discounts. If you cannot tell what has been done, ask, and ask whether there are receipts. My post on [homeowners insurance in the Foothills](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/foothills-homeowners-insurance-after-eaton-fire) explains why it matters to your premium.

## Inside: Look Past the Staging

**Water damage.** Check ceilings and walls for stains, rings, bubbling paint, and warped floors, and notice any musty smell. Bring a flashlight and look up, since ceilings are easy to skip, and watch for past repairs. Fresh paint and staging can hide signs of water intrusion, so one oddly fresh patch on a ceiling is a good question for the host.

**Movement.** Open and close doors and windows. Sticking doors, gaps around frames, sloping floors, and diagonal wall cracks can point to movement. Some cracks are harmless, but these signs deserve a professional look, and that is exactly what your inspection is for.

**Water pressure.** Your inspector will test the fixtures properly later, so keep it light at an open house. If it is easy to do, [turn on a tap](https://rismedia.com/?p=242569) to check the pressure, and ask the host first if you are not sure it is okay. Skip the toilets. Weak pressure or slow drains are worth a note.

**Big-ticket systems.** Find the water heater, furnace, and AC unit. They are often in a closet, garage, side yard, or attic. The label usually lists the make and model, and the serial number often points to the age, which your inspector can help decode. According to [InterNACHI's estimates](https://www.nachi.org/life-expectancy.htm), a conventional water heater lasts about 6 to 12 years, a central AC unit 7 to 15, and a furnace 15 to 25. A system near the end of its range is not a dealbreaker. It is a number to add to your budget, and it can be useful when negotiating the price.

**Light, sound, and layout.** [Turn the lights off to judge the natural light](https://rismedia.com/?p=242569). Stand still for a moment and listen for traffic. Picture your furniture, your storage, and how you would move through the kitchen on a busy morning.

**Additions.** If a room feels added on, or a garage looks converted, ask whether the work was permitted. Renovations that skipped permits can impact appraisal numbers.

One etiquette note: opening closets and cabinet doors is generally fair game. Dresser drawers and medicine cabinets of occupied homes are not.

## Questions to Ask the Agent Hosting

The host represents the seller and may not be able to answer everything. For anything that matters, ask where the answer is documented. Written disclosures are better than a hallway conversation, and that is a habit worth keeping.

- Is there an offer deadline, or have offers come in?
- Is the disclosure package available to review, and what is in it? My guide to [California seller disclosures](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/california-seller-disclosures-tds-spq-avid-nhd) explains each form.
- What work has been done recently, and was it permitted?
- When were the roof, water heater, AC, and furnace last replaced or serviced?
- Is the home in a fire hazard zone, and what hardening has been done?
- If there is an HOA, what are the dues, and are the documents available? My [guide to buying a condo](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/buying-a-condo-los-angeles-hoa-guide) covers what to look for.
- Does the seller have a preferred closing date, or want to stay after closing with a [rent-back](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/rent-back-agreement-selling-before-buying-california)?

It's entirely possible the host won't have the answers to all of these questions, and that's ok. Send (or have your agent send) an email recapping the answers you received to confirm accuracy, and ask any outstanding questions in that email.

## After You Leave

Take five minutes in the car to write down your top three likes and top three concerns while they are fresh. If you are seeing several homes in a weekend, they blur together fast.

If a home earns a second look, ask your agent about a private showing and when to expect the disclosures. They are not always available right away, and sometimes they arrive once negotiations are underway. When they do, read them closely, because [California gives buyers a window to cancel when a required disclosure arrives after the offer is made](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/california-seller-disclosures-tds-spq-avid-nhd). From there, your protection is your inspection period, which I break down in my post on [real estate contingencies](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/what-are-real-estate-contingencies).

Before you write an offer, have your [pre-approval](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/pre-approval-vs-pre-qualification) in hand. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need an agent to go to an open house?

No. According to NAR's consumer guide, you do not need to sign a written buyer agreement just to visit an open house on your own or to ask about an agent's services. Keep in mind that the agent hosting the open house typically represents the seller.

### Do I have to sign the sign-in sheet at an open house?

No. Guidance from the California Association of REALTORS® says signing the visitor sign-in form is not a condition of entering an open house. If you do sign, expect a follow-up from the host, and tell them if you already work with an agent.

### Can I take photos at an open house?

Ask the agent hosting first. It is the seller's home, and some sellers prefer no photos, so asking is good manners.

### What are the biggest red flags to look for at an open house?

Water stains or musty smells, doors and windows that stick, sloping floors or diagonal wall cracks, and an aging roof or HVAC system are common reasons for a closer look. A red flag is a reason to ask questions and have an inspector look, not automatically a reason to walk away.

## The Bottom Line

Every home has something. The goal is not to find a perfect house. It is to spot the flaws you can price in, plan for, or live with, and to notice the ones you cannot, before you are emotionally attached to a kitchen island.
