Building an ADU before you sell can sound like an easy way to add value to your home. But it is not quite that simple.

I get asked about this a lot by sellers in La Crescenta and La Cañada who have larger, buildable lots. The question is usually some version of: Should I build the ADU before I sell, or should I let the next owner take that project on?

It is a good question. And before you spend six figures on a construction project, I think it is worth looking at the actual numbers, the timeline, and what buyers are likely to pay for it.

## The short answer

An ADU can absolutely add value to your property. But that does not automatically mean building one before you sell is the best financial decision.

You need three things to line up:

1. The lot needs to support the project.
2. You need enough time to actually complete it.
3. The value it adds needs to justify what you are spending to build it.

If you are planning to sell in the next 60 to 90 days, I would almost never recommend starting an ADU project just for the sake of increasing your sale price. You are looking at months of planning, permitting, construction, inspections, and potential surprises.

In that situation, I would rather help you document the property's ADU potential so buyers can see the opportunity for themselves.

If you have a longer timeline, though, it is absolutely worth running the numbers.

## What does an ADU cost to build right now?

This is where the conversation gets real pretty quickly.

ADU costs vary quite a bit depending on the size, whether you are converting an existing garage, and what your particular lot requires. Current [Los Angeles-area estimates](https://www.greatbuildz.com/blog/cost-to-build-an-adu-in-los-angeles/) are roughly:

- Garage conversion: $120,000 to $150,000
- Detached 400 to 500 square feet: $150,000 to $200,000
- Detached 600 to 800 square feet: $200,000 to $250,000
- Detached 800 to 1,000 square feet: $250,000 to $300,000
- Detached 1,000 to 1,200 square feet or two-story: $300,000 to $400,000

And construction is not the only expense.

You may also have several thousand dollars in architectural and design costs, along with permitting, plan review, utility work, and other city fees.

So when someone tells you, "We can build an ADU for $150,000," I would want to know exactly what that number includes before using it to make a decision.

The last thing you want is to decide that an ADU makes financial sense based on a construction estimate that does not include half of the actual project.

## How much value does an ADU really add?

This is the part that gets a little more complicated.

A permitted, well-built ADU can add meaningful value to a property, particularly in areas where buyers see the potential for rental income, multigenerational living, a home office, or additional private space. Studies on California ADUs commonly point to [value gains in the 20 to 30 percent range](https://www.apex-homes.us/blog/how-much-value-does-an-adu-add-to-a-home-in-california/) for the property overall, though that range moves a lot depending on the property.

But I would be careful about assuming that every dollar you spend comes back to you when you sell.

An ADU is not like putting $100,000 into the house and automatically getting $100,000 added to the sale price (and even if it was, it would have to really open up your market audience to a totally new segment to make that break-even move worth the headache).

The value depends on the property, the neighborhood, the size and quality of the ADU, and what buyers in that particular market are willing to pay.

Appraisers can look at comparable properties with ADUs, and in some situations they can also consider the income-producing potential of the unit. But the numbers need to make sense for the property as a whole.

A $300,000 ADU does not necessarily make a $1 million house worth $1.3 million.

That is why I would want to look at your specific property before telling you that building one is a good investment.

And if you are considering keeping the property as a rental instead of selling right away, the calculation changes again. That is where the financing and rental-income side of the equation becomes important. I wrote more about that in [Multifamily Financing Basics](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/multifamily-financing-basics).

## What happens to your property taxes?

This is another question I hear from homeowners who are considering an ADU, especially people who have owned their homes for a long time.

The good news is that building an ADU does not generally mean the entire property gets reassessed for property tax purposes.

Under [Prop 13](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/prop-13-lock-in-effect-selling-california), your existing home keeps its existing assessed value and the associated annual limitations on increases. The newly constructed ADU is assessed separately.

So, as a very rough example, if the assessor determines that your new ADU has a value of $300,000, you could be looking at roughly $3,000 per year in additional property taxes, depending on the final assessed value and local tax rate.

That is obviously something to factor into the decision, but it is very different from having the entire property reassessed at today's market value.

If the property came to you through an inheritance or parent-child transfer, there is another layer to consider. Prop 19 has its own rules about how the existing property tax basis transfers. I covered that separately in [Prop 19 and Parent-Child Transfers](https://closingwithcaila.com/blog/prop-19-parent-child-property-tax-transfer-california).

## The timeline is the part sellers sometimes underestimate

This is probably the biggest reason I would hesitate to recommend an ADU as a pre-sale project when someone is already thinking about listing.

California law requires most complete ADU applications to be approved within a set timeframe, but approval is not the same thing as having a finished ADU in your backyard.

You still have design and planning, plan review, permits, utility connections, inspections, construction, and all the little things that tend to happen once a construction project gets underway.

In a perfect world, everything moves along exactly as planned. Real life is usually a little different.

If you have six months, a year, or longer before you need to sell, that may be a perfectly reasonable project to consider.

If you told me, "I want to put my house on the market in 60 days," I would not tell you to start building an ADU. At that point, I would rather focus on making the property as attractive as possible to the buyers who are actually going to be shopping for it (think: repairs, improvements on existing space, landscaping, staging).

## What if you already have an unpermitted ADU?

This comes up more often than you might think in older Foothills neighborhoods.

Maybe someone enclosed a garage years ago. Maybe a previous owner added a bathroom or kitchen. Maybe there is a structure in the backyard that has been used as living space for years but was never properly permitted.

It does not typically mean you cannot sell the property.

It does mean we need to deal with it honestly. Known [unpermitted work needs to be disclosed](https://www.lametrohomefinder.com/blog/sell-house-unpermitted-work-los-angeles), and there can be financing and appraisal implications depending on what was done.

My own first house actually had an unpermitted ADU on the property. Because it was disclosed properly, it added value without adding complication. That is how it should go when it is handled right, which is exactly why I do not want you panicking over this if it applies to you.

From there, you generally have a few options.

**Legalize it**

If the work qualifies, bringing an unpermitted ADU into compliance can make the property much easier to market and can potentially expand your buyer pool.

California has also created pathways for certain older unpermitted ADUs to be legalized under more favorable rules. If your ADU was built before January 1, 2020, it is worth us finding out whether it qualifies before assuming the process will be expensive or complicated.

**Sell it as-is**

Sometimes the best decision is to disclose the work, price the property accordingly, and let the buyer decide what they want to do.

The tradeoff is that some buyers and lenders may have concerns about the unpermitted space, which can affect your buyer pool and the offers you receive. This needs to be assessed on a neighborhood-by-neighborhood (or honestly: a block-by-block) basis by a realtor who knows the market.

**Sell to a cash buyer**

This can be the fastest route in a situation where there are significant permitting issues or a very short timeline. It can also mean accepting a lower price in exchange for speed and a simpler transaction.

There is no universal "correct" answer here (there never is, right?). It depends on what is unpermitted, what it would take to legalize it, and how quickly you need to sell.

## So, should you build an ADU before you sell?

Sorry, another no universal correct answer situation. So I've got an annoying "maybe!" answer for you until we [sit down and talk through your specific situation](https://closingwithcaila.com/contact).

If you have a large lot, a reasonable construction budget, plenty of time, and a property where buyers are likely to see real value in an ADU, it can be a smart investment.

If you are trying to sell in the next couple of months, I would probably take a different approach.

Instead of spending hundreds of thousands of dollars and hoping the market gives you that money back, we can look at the property's existing ADU potential and figure out how to communicate that opportunity to buyers.

Sometimes the smartest improvement you can make before selling is not building something at all. It is making sure buyers understand what is already possible.

And if you already have an unpermitted ADU, do not panic. It is something we can work through. The important thing is to understand your options before you put the property on the market.

## FAQ

**Does building an ADU actually increase my home's sale price?**
Usually, yes. A permitted, well-built ADU can add meaningful value, especially when it fits the property and what buyers in the area are looking for.

But I would not assume you will get back every dollar you spend. The value depends on your property, the neighborhood, the size and quality of the ADU, and current buyer demand.

**How much does it cost to build an ADU in La Crescenta, La Cañada, or Glendale?**
Current Los Angeles-area estimates range from roughly $120,000 for a garage conversion to $300,000 or more for a larger detached unit. Design, permitting, utility work, and other project costs can add significantly to the total.

The estimate that matters, though, is the one for your lot.

**Will building an ADU trigger a full property tax reassessment?**
Generally, no. The existing home is not typically reassessed at current market value simply because you add an ADU. The new ADU is assessed separately.

If your property has a complicated tax history, such as an inherited property or a Prop 19 transfer, it is worth looking at that situation separately.

**What if I already have an unpermitted ADU and want to sell?**
You have options.

Depending on the age and circumstances of the ADU, you may be able to legalize it. You can also sell the property as-is with the required disclosures, or consider a cash buyer if speed is the priority.

The right choice depends on your timeline and what it would actually cost to bring the space into compliance.

**Is it better to sell as-is and let the buyer build the ADU?**
If you are trying to sell within the next 60 to 90 days, I would seriously consider it.

Rather than taking on a major construction project yourself, you may be better off documenting the property's ADU potential and making sure buyers understand what they could do with the lot.

Sometimes the best way to add value before a sale is to show buyers the value that is already there.
